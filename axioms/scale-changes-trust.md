# Scale changes the mechanics of trust

## Claim

Trust models from small groups do not scale linearly to communities of tens of thousands. Large communities produce trust through legible process, consistent enforcement, and visible legitimacy — not through interpersonal knowledge.

## Justification

In a small community, trust is produced by knowing each other. Members build reputation over time through direct interaction; disputes are resolved through relationships; norms are enforced through social pressure from people you recognize. This works because the network is small enough for every member to hold a working model of most others. Dunbar's (1992) work on the cognitive limits of stable social relationships — typically cited at around 150 for humans — is a useful upper bound on where this interpersonal mechanism can operate without aid.

In communities too large for any member to know more than a fraction of the others, this mechanism fails. Members cannot verify individually who can be trusted, so they rely on *process* to tell them whether the community as a whole can be trusted. Do moderators act consistently? Are decisions explained? Does the stated code match the observed enforcement? Are disagreements handled transparently or buried? These process signals substitute for the interpersonal signals that worked at smaller scales. Ostrom (1990), studying commons governance across centuries and continents, identified clear rules, graduated sanctions, monitoring, and accessible conflict resolution as recurring design principles in long-lived collective institutions — the same kind of legible process that online communities at scale require. Kraut and Resnick (2012), synthesizing the empirical literature on online community design, reach compatible conclusions: at scale, evidence-based social design substitutes for the interpersonal trust that small groups enjoy by default.

This is a qualitative shift, not a quantitative one. A small community run on interpersonal trust, scaled up, does not become a large community running on interpersonal trust. It becomes a large community where trust is breaking down, because the substrate has failed. Established members may still feel the old mechanism is working — they still know the other established members — while newer and peripheral members experience a community where nothing legible tells them whom to trust or what will happen when things go wrong. Deliberate investment in legible process is the alternative substrate, and it has to be built before it is needed.

## Implications

- Governance at scale must produce and broadcast process signals: documented rules, documented decisions, audit trails, consistent enforcement, explicit appeals.
- "We just know each other" is not available as a trust mechanism at scale. Attempts to rely on it produce insiderism that excludes the peripheral majority.
- Growth is not neutral. Communities that grow past the scale where their original trust mechanisms worked must explicitly retool, or they will suffer legitimacy erosion even if nothing else changes.
- Process-based trust feels colder and more bureaucratic than interpersonal trust. This is a cost worth paying; the alternative is not warmer trust, it is the absence of trust for most members.
