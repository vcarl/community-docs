# Affiliation is mostly peripheral

## Claim

The typical relationship to a large community is lurking, occasional participation, or self-identification — not active contribution. Governance models that address only contributors address only a minority of the people the community affects and is affected by.

## Justification

Repeated empirical observation across online communities finds that most members read much more than they write, and most who write do so rarely. Nonnecke and Preece (2000), surveying mailing list participation, documented that the majority of subscribers to most lists never posted, and that lurking is not a precursor to participation but a stable mode of membership in its own right. In a community of tens of thousands, the contributing core is a few hundred; the participating layer is a few thousand; the remaining tens of thousands are lurkers, claimers of affiliation, and peripheral users. Their relationship to the community is real — they read, they shape it by reading, they form opinions, they speak on its behalf in other contexts — but it is not legible to a contribution-based governance model.

Lave and Wenger's (1991) concept of *legitimate peripheral participation* names a related dynamic from the study of communities of practice: peripheral participants are not failed full participants but are engaged in a different, valid mode of membership. For some, peripherality is a stage; for others it is the destination. Either way, peripheral members are part of the community and are shaped by it — and shape it in return.

Existing governance frameworks, especially in open source, tend to center contributors or official representatives. Codes of Conduct often apply within "community spaces" or when someone is "representing the project." This leaves most actual community members and most actual community-adjacent behavior outside the frame. The Mozilla "claiming affiliation" clause and the professional-association model (ACM, IEEE, where membership itself is the basis of agreement) gesture at broader coverage, but enforcement and engagement mechanisms for peripheral members remain genuinely underdeveloped.

Peripheral affiliation is not a deficient form of contribution waiting to mature. It is the normal condition of membership at scale. A healthy large community is one where peripheral relationships are sustainable, legible, and valued in their own terms, not treated as a funnel into a contributor pipeline.

## Implications

- Governance artifacts (rules, decisions, norms, processes) must be legible to peripheral members, not only to the contributing core.
- Member-engagement work should design for the reader as a first-class participant, not as a future contributor waiting to activate.
- The question "whose community is this?" has a much larger answer than "who contributes to this?"
- Legitimacy-building efforts that only reach the contributing layer will be invisible to most of the community they are meant to serve.
