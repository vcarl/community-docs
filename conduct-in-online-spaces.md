# Moderation enforcement: What works, what doesn't

Rule posting and removal explanations. [Matias (2019)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1813486116) and [Jhaver (2019)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3359252) show enormous effect sizes from lightweight interventions — just making rules visible reduces violations and increases participation. Jhaver's finding that only 0.6% of subreddits provide removal explanations suggests massive untapped potential.

Suspensions work, and longer is better — but with diminishing returns. [Gleason (2025)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713163) provides the first causal evidence that duration matters, particularly for first-time offenders.

Permanent bans function more as rhetoric than rehabilitation. [Kou (2021)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3476075) argues they define community boundaries rather than reform behavior.

Community bans are effective for the banning platform but create externalities. [Chandrasekharan (2017)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3134666) shows 80%+ hate speech reduction on Reddit; [Ribeiro (2021)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3476057) shows the migrated communities shrink but become more extreme. Russo (2024) reveals a bimodal distribution — most users improve slightly, but a tail becomes significantly worse.

Quarantines are weak. They slow recruitment but don't change existing user behavior ([Jhaver 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3490499)).

Restorative approaches are theoretically promising but empirically untested at scale. [Schoenebeck & Blackwell (2021)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3895779) provide the theoretical framework; implementation research is still emerging.

Norms beat punishments for long-term culture. Seering (2019) finds that moderators who engage personally during incidents — rather than relying on automated enforcement — build stronger community norms.
