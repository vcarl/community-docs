# Codes of conduct in Open Source Software — for warm and fuzzy feelings or equality in community?

**Authors:** Vandana Singh, Brice Bongiovanni, William Brandon
**Published:** 2021, Software Quality Journal, Vol. 30, pp. 581-620
**DOI:** 10.1007/s11219-020-09543-w
**Link:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11219-020-09543-w

## Abstract

This paper focuses on codes of conduct (CoC) of Free and Open Source Software (F/OSS) and their role in improving women's participation in online communities of OSS. We investigated 355 F/OSS software package websites for the presence of codes of conduct and then analyzed these for diversity elements. Qualitative content analysis of the websites shows that less than 10% (28) of the analyzed websites had some type of community rules such as CoC. In-depth analysis of the CoCs demonstrated many discrepancies in the terminology, length, enforcement, and adoption of codes of conduct. To investigate the usage of these CoCs, we investigate five women-focused OSS discussion forums. This analysis shows the value of creating/adopting a CoC and the impact CoC can have on the participation of women. We also present the challenges in the usage and enforcement of CoCs as discussed by women of these forums. We conclude with recommendations for better enforcement of CoC and reflection on the ethical underpinnings of CoC as a tool to improve diversity and inclusion in OSS.

## AI Summary

This study takes a diversity-focused lens to examine codes of conduct in free and open source software (F/OSS), specifically investigating their role in women's participation. The research uses a two-phase mixed-methods design.

In the first phase, the researchers conducted a systematic survey of 355 F/OSS software package websites to determine the prevalence of codes of conduct and community guidelines. They performed a quantitative content analysis to identify the presence and characteristics of CoCs, finding that fewer than 10% (only 28 of 355) of the analyzed websites had any form of community behavioral rules. This alarmingly low adoption rate highlights how few OSS projects had formalized behavioral expectations at the time of the study.

For the projects that did have CoCs, the researchers conducted an in-depth qualitative content analysis examining multiple dimensions: the terminology used (e.g., "code of conduct" vs. "community guidelines" vs. "participation guidelines"), document length and detail, the specificity of enforcement mechanisms, and whether the CoC explicitly addressed diversity and inclusion. They found significant discrepancies across all dimensions — some CoCs were brief aspirational statements while others were detailed governance documents with clear enforcement procedures.

In the second phase, the researchers analyzed discussions in five women-focused OSS forums (communities specifically created to support women in open source). This qualitative analysis examined how women in OSS discuss and experience codes of conduct, revealing both the perceived value and the practical challenges of CoCs. Forum participants reported that CoCs served important functions: signaling that a project values inclusion, providing a framework for reporting harassment, and setting behavioral expectations for newcomers. However, they also identified significant challenges: inconsistent enforcement, lack of designated enforcement contacts, lack of transparency in how reports are handled, and the emotional labor imposed on women who must advocate for CoC creation and enforcement.

The study's conclusions emphasize that the mere existence of a CoC is insufficient — enforcement is the critical factor determining whether a CoC actually improves the climate for underrepresented contributors. The researchers offer recommendations including: clearly designating enforcement contacts, establishing transparent reporting and resolution processes, training community leaders in conflict resolution, and regularly reviewing and updating CoC language. They also reflect on the ethical foundations of CoCs, arguing that they should be understood not just as governance tools but as expressions of community values around equity and inclusion.

A key insight is the gap between CoC adoption and CoC effectiveness: even among the small minority of projects with CoCs, enforcement quality varied enormously. The paper contributes to a growing body of evidence suggesting that CoCs function primarily as signaling mechanisms — and that the signal only remains credible if backed by consistent, visible enforcement.
