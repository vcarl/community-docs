# Becoming Wikipedian: Transformation of Participation in a Collaborative Online Encyclopedia

Bryant, S. L., Forte, A., & Bruckman, A. (2005). Becoming Wikipedian: Transformation of Participation in a Collaborative Online Encyclopedia. *Proceedings of GROUP 2005*, 1–10. https://doi.org/10.1145/1099203.1099205

## Abstract

Traditional activities change in surprising ways when computer-mediated communication becomes a component of the activity system. In this descriptive study, we leverage two perspectives on social activity to understand the experiences of individuals who became active collaborators in Wikipedia, a prolific, cooperatively-authored online encyclopedia. Legitimate peripheral participation provides a lens for understanding participation in a community as an adaptable process that evolves over time. We use ideas from activity theory as a framework to describe our results. Finally, we describe how activity on the Wikipedia stands in striking contrast to traditional publishing and suggests a new paradigm for collaborative systems.

## AI Summary

Bryant, Forte, and Bruckman (2005) offer one of the first sustained qualitative accounts of how individuals come to identify as committed members of Wikipedia, drawing on interviews with experienced editors to trace the path from tentative first edits to deep investment in the project. The paper is explicitly theoretical in framing: it reads Wikipedia through Lave and Wenger's concept of legitimate peripheral participation, supplemented by activity theory, and argues that what Wikipedia supplies is not merely an editable text but a structured arena in which a newcomer's relationship to the community, its tools, and its purposes can gradually reorganize itself over time.

The empirical core of the paper is a set of in-depth interviews with active Wikipedians, chosen for having moved beyond casual contribution into sustained engagement. The authors ask how these individuals first encountered the site, what drew them in, and how their sense of what they were doing changed as they kept contributing. The recurring pattern is a trajectory rather than a recruitment event. Participants typically began as readers, drifted into making small corrections — fixing a typo, adding a missing fact, adjusting a sentence in an article they happened to know something about — and found, sometimes to their own surprise, that these low-stakes acts pulled them into a widening set of activities. Over time, the scope of what they attended to expanded from the single article in front of them to categories of articles, then to the maintenance work that keeps the encyclopedia coherent, and finally to the meta-work of policy discussion, dispute resolution, and community stewardship.

Crucially, the authors describe this expansion as a reorientation of goals rather than an accumulation of skills. Early contributions are oriented toward the article; later contributions are oriented toward the encyclopedia as an ongoing project; still later, experienced contributors describe orienting themselves toward the community itself, treating the health of the collaborative process as a primary object of their attention. Participants' understanding of what counts as a contribution shifts in step with this reorientation. Writing prose comes to share space with watchlisting, reverting vandalism, arbitrating talk-page disputes, and socializing newcomers — activities that would have seemed opaque or uninteresting at the start. The authors read this shift as characteristic of movement from peripheral toward fuller participation in Wenger's sense: not a promotion, but a change in what the participant is trying to do and in how they locate themselves within the collective endeavor.

The authors draw attention to the role of unwritten understanding in this trajectory. Their interviewees describe learning how Wikipedia works less by reading policies than by watching, trying things, being corrected, and absorbing — through extended exposure — a feel for which edits survive, which disputes are worth entering, and what the community treats as acceptable. Formal policy pages exist and are consulted, but the texture of membership is built out of countless small encounters that accrete into a practical sense of the place. The gradual and partly inarticulate character of this learning is, in the authors' framing, not a deficiency of onboarding but a constitutive feature of how identification with the community develops. The movement from peripheral to committed participation takes time precisely because there is something to be absorbed that cannot be handed over on arrival.

The paper closes by arguing that Wikipedia's model contrasts sharply with traditional publishing, where the line between producer and audience is fixed and the path between them is institutional. In Wikipedia, that line is permeable and the path is self-directed: a reader can become an editor, an editor can become an administrator, and an administrator can shape policy, each transition emerging from continued participation rather than from credential or appointment. The authors present this permeability — and the extended developmental arc it supports — as the distinctive shape of the activity system they studied.
