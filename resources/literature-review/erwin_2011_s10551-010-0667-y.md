# Corporate Codes of Conduct: The Effects of Code Content and Quality on Ethical Performance

**Authors:** Patrick M. Erwin
**Published:** 2011, Journal of Business Ethics, Vol. 99, pp. 535-548
**DOI:** 10.1007/s10551-010-0667-y
**Link:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-010-0667-y

## Abstract

Corporate codes of conduct are a practical corporate social responsibility (CSR) instrument commonly used to govern employee behavior and establish a socially responsible organizational culture. The effectiveness of these codes has been widely discussed on theoretical grounds and empirically tested in numerous previous reports that directly compare companies with and without codes of conduct. Empirical research has yielded inconsistent results that may be explained by multiple ancillary factors, including the quality of code content and implementation, which are excluded from analyses based solely on the presence or absence of codes. This study investigated the importance of code content in determining code effectiveness by examining the relationship between code of conduct quality and ethical performance. Companies maintaining high quality codes of conduct were significantly more represented among top CSR ranking systems for corporate citizenship, sustainability, ethical behavior, and public perception. Further, a significant relationship was observed between code quality and CSR performance, across a full range of ethical rankings. These findings suggest code quality may play a crucial role in the effectiveness of codes of conduct and their ability to transform organizational cultures. Future research efforts should transcend traditional comparisons based on the presence or absence of ethical codes and begin to examine the essential factors leading to the effective establishment of CSR policies and sustainable business practices in corporate culture.

## AI Summary

This study directly responds to the methodological critique raised by Kaptein and Schwartz (2008) by moving beyond simple presence/absence comparisons. Instead of asking "does having a code matter?", Erwin asks "does the quality of a code matter?"

The methodology involves evaluating the content quality of corporate codes of conduct and correlating code quality scores with companies' rankings on multiple CSR performance indices, including the 100 Best Corporate Citizens (BCC) list, the Dow Jones Sustainability Index (DJSI), the World's Most Ethical Companies (WMEC) list, and the World's Most Respected Companies (WMRC) list.

Erwin developed a code quality assessment framework drawing on prior taxonomies (Gaumnitz & Lere 2002, 2004) to score codes on dimensions including: specificity of behavioral guidelines, breadth of stakeholder coverage, clarity of enforcement mechanisms, and comprehensiveness of ethical topics addressed. Codes ranged from brief, vague aspirational statements to detailed documents with specific behavioral standards and enforcement procedures.

The key finding is that code quality — not mere code presence — significantly predicts ethical performance. Companies with high-quality codes were significantly overrepresented in top CSR rankings across all four ranking systems examined. The relationship held across the full range of rankings, not just at the extremes.

This has direct implications for online community codes of conduct: it suggests that the specific content, specificity, and quality of a CoC matters more than simply having one. A boilerplate CoC adopted without customization may have little effect, while a carefully crafted one with specific behavioral standards and clear enforcement procedures may meaningfully influence community behavior. The study bridges corporate ethics research and community governance by demonstrating that codes are not interchangeable — their design characteristics matter for outcomes.
