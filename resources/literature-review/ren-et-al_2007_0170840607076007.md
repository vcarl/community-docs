# Applying Common Identity and Bond Theory to Design of Online Communities

Ren, Y., Kraut, R., & Kiesler, S. (2007). Applying Common Identity and Bond Theory to Design of Online Communities. *Organization Studies*, 28(3), 377–408. https://doi.org/10.1177/0170840607076007

## Abstract

Online communities depend upon the commitment and voluntary participation of their members. Community design — site navigation, community structure and features, and organizational policies — is critical in this regard. Community design affects how people can interact, the information they receive about one another and the community, and how they can participate in community activities. We argue that the constraints and opportunities inherent in online community design influence how people become attached to the community and whether they are willing to expend effort on its behalf. We examine two theories of group attachment and link these theories with design decisions for online communities. Common identity theory makes predictions about the causes and consequences of people's attachment to the group as a whole. Common bond theory makes predictions about the causes and consequences of people's attachment to individual group members. We review causes of common identity and common bond, and show how they result in different kinds of attachment and group outcomes. We then show how design decisions, such as those focused on recruiting newcomers versus retaining existing members, constraining or promoting off-topic discussion, and limiting group size or allowing uncontrolled growth, can lead to common identity or interpersonal bonds among community members, and consequently to different levels and forms of community participation by those so motivated.

## AI Summary

Ren, Kraut, and Kiesler (2007) import a distinction from small-group social psychology into the design literature on online communities: the difference between *identity-based* attachment and *bond-based* attachment. The distinction, originally developed by Prentice, Miller, and Lightdale and rooted in work by Turner and colleagues, separates two kinds of "belonging." In identity-based attachment, members are attached to the group *as a category* — to its topic, purpose, or collective self-definition — and other members are largely interchangeable instances of that category. In bond-based attachment, members are attached to *specific other people* whom they have come to know and care about; the group matters because those individuals are in it. The authors' contribution is to treat these as distinct causal pathways with distinct design antecedents and distinct behavioral consequences, rather than as two phrasings of the same underlying affiliation.

The causes diverge sharply. **Identity-based attachment** is produced by conditions that make the group category salient and coherent: a clear and visible topic or mission, shared symbols and norms, homogeneity of content, contrast with out-groups, and information that depicts the community *as a community* rather than as a set of individuals. A newcomer encountering such a community can form an attachment to what the group *is* — its subject matter and collective identity — without knowing any particular member. **Bond-based attachment**, by contrast, is produced by conditions that support repeated, personalized interaction between specific individuals: small-group settings, sustained dyadic exchange, self-disclosure, member profiles that reveal personality and history, and interaction structures that let participants recognize and return to one another over time. Identity depends on *category salience*; bonds depend on *person salience*.

The consequences also diverge. Identity-based attachment supports contributions aimed at the collective good — adherence to group norms, production of public content, recruitment of new members who fit the category, and loyalty that survives turnover in membership. Bond-based attachment supports contributions aimed at particular others — mutual support, responsiveness, retention of named individuals — but is more fragile under departure of those individuals and is more easily disrupted by growth that dilutes the dyadic texture of interaction.

From this theoretical frame the authors derive a set of design implications. Communities seeking identity-based attachment benefit from homogeneity and focus: tight topical scope, visible markers of group membership, moderation that suppresses off-topic drift, and presentations of aggregate activity that render the collective visible to its members. Communities seeking bond-based attachment benefit from features that make individuals legible and reachable: member profiles, persistent identities, reputation displays, small-group or sub-group structures, and mechanisms that surface interaction history between specific pairs. Scale enters as a design variable rather than a neutral condition. Large, loosely topical communities tend to produce identity attachment because person-level knowledge cannot be sustained across the membership; small, interaction-rich communities tend to produce bond attachment because category-level salience weakens when the group is too intimate to feel like a category. Design choices about recruitment, retention, off-topic tolerance, and growth push toward one regime or the other, often without designers recognizing the choice.

A feature of the argument that bears emphasis is that the two modes are not stages on a single developmental path. A member whose attachment is identity-based is not a bond-based member in waiting; the attachment has its own sources, its own behavioral signature, and its own design logic. A community may be populated largely by members for whom the group-as-category is the object of attachment — who read, identify, and occasionally contribute without forming personal ties to other members — and such membership is a stable configuration produced by the community's design, not a failure of the community to cultivate something deeper. The authors present the two attachment types as parallel alternatives, each coherent in its own right, each produced by identifiable mechanisms, and each supported or undermined by specific design decisions.
