# Comparing the Perceived Legitimacy of Content Moderation Processes: Contractors, Algorithms, Expert Panels, and Digital Juries

Pan, C. A., Yakhmi, S., Iyer, T. P., Strasnick, E., Zhang, A. X., & Bernstein, M. S. (2022). Comparing the Perceived Legitimacy of Content Moderation Processes: Contractors, Algorithms, Expert Panels, and Digital Juries. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 6(CSCW1), Article 82. https://doi.org/10.1145/3512929

## Abstract

While research continues to investigate and improve the accuracy, fairness, and normative appropriateness of content moderation processes on large social media platforms, even the best process cannot be effective if users reject its authority as illegitimate. We present a survey experiment comparing the perceived institutional legitimacy of four popular content moderation processes. We conducted a within-subjects experiment in which we showed US Facebook users moderation decisions and randomized the description of whether those decisions were made by paid contractors, algorithms, expert panels, or juries of users. Prior work suggests that juries will have the highest perceived legitimacy due to the benefits of judicial independence and democratic representation. However, expert panels had greater perceived legitimacy than algorithms or juries. Moreover, outcome alignment - agreement with the decision - played a larger role than process in determining perceived legitimacy. These results suggest benefits to incorporating expert oversight in content moderation and underscore that any process will face legitimacy challenges derived from disagreement about outcomes.

## AI Summary

Pan and colleagues (2022) take a question that much of the content-moderation literature had approached normatively — which governance arrangement is *legitimate* — and convert it into one that can be measured. Legitimacy, in their framing, is a perception held by third-party observers: a moderation decision is legitimate to the extent that people who see it rendered are willing to accept the authority of the body that rendered it, independent of whether they agree with the particular outcome. The paper's contribution is to treat that perception as an experimentally manipulable variable and to ask, holding the underlying decision constant, *whether the identity of the decision-maker changes how the decision is received*.

The design is a within-subjects survey experiment with U.S. Facebook users. Participants were shown actual-looking moderation decisions — content that had been removed or allowed to stand — and, for each decision, the authors randomized the description of *who* had made it. Four process conditions were contrasted: paid commercial-moderation contractors (the dominant industry arrangement), automated algorithms, expert panels drawn from people with relevant professional standing, and digital juries composed of ordinary users empaneled to adjudicate the case. Because each respondent saw multiple conditions, between-person differences in general trust or political orientation could be absorbed, and the authors could isolate the effect of *process attribution* on legitimacy judgments about an otherwise identical decision.

The results disturb an assumption that had gathered force in prior normative work. That literature had predicted digital juries would win on legitimacy, drawing on the procedural-justice tradition and on the democratic intuition that decisions about speech among users ought to be made by users. Pan and colleagues do find that juries are rated more legitimate than the status-quo arrangement — paid contractors — and more legitimate than purely algorithmic decision-making, which tended to fare worst. But juries did not top the ranking. Expert panels did. Observers, shown the same decisions, judged them more legitimate when attributed to a panel of experts than when attributed to a jury of peers, to contractors, or to an algorithm. The democratic-representation argument for juries turned out to capture only part of what observers were weighing; the perceived competence and standing conferred by expertise carried independent weight.

A second finding runs alongside the process comparison and partly qualifies it. Whether a respondent agreed with the *outcome* of a given decision — whether they thought the content in question should have been removed or left up — exerted a larger influence on legitimacy ratings than the process attribution did. Process mattered, and mattered in a structured way, but outcome alignment mattered more. This is consistent with a long-standing tension in the procedural-justice tradition: the procedures through which decisions are reached shape acceptance, but they do not override substantive disagreement. A body whose rulings an observer dislikes will be judged less legitimate than one whose rulings the observer endorses, even when the procedural form is held constant.

Taken together, the study offers an empirical test of procedural predictions in a platform governance setting and produces a more complicated picture than either the "algorithms are efficient" or "let users decide" positions had anticipated. Processes incorporating deliberation by identifiable human decision-makers — whether panels of experts or juries of users — are perceived as more legitimate than the prevailing contractor and algorithmic arrangements, with expert panels leading. And every arrangement, however it is constituted, remains vulnerable to the fact that people who disagree with a ruling will judge the body that issued it more harshly. Process design can raise the baseline of perceived legitimacy; it cannot, on the evidence here, insulate a governance body from the reactive effect of substantive disagreement with its decisions.
