# Interactional Coherence in CMC

Herring, S. C. (1999). Interactional Coherence in CMC. *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*, 4(4). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1083-6101.1999.tb00106.x

## Abstract

Text-only CMC has been claimed to be interactionally incoherent due to limitations imposed by messaging systems on turn-taking and reference, yet its popularity continues to grow. In an attempt to resolve this apparent paradox, this study evaluates the coherence of computer-mediated interaction by surveying research on cross-turn coherence. The results reveal a high degree of disrupted adjacency, overlapping exchanges, and topic decay. Two explanations are proposed to account for the popularity of CMC despite its relative incoherence: the ability of users to adapt to the medium, and the advantages of loosened coherence for heightened interactivity and language play.

## AI Summary

Herring (1999) approaches computer-mediated communication with the analytic apparatus of conversation analysis and discourse coherence, asking a question that the popularity of chat systems had made awkward for linguists: if text-based CMC is structurally incoherent by the standards that describe spoken interaction, why do people persist in using it, and in fact prefer it for certain kinds of exchange? Her answer requires first documenting the incoherence carefully, then accounting for how participants nonetheless manage to make sense of what happens.

The documentation is the analytical heart of the paper. Herring examines how turn-taking operates in synchronous text chat — primarily IRC-style systems and comparable environments — and shows that the orderly machinery Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson described for face-to-face talk does not transfer. In spoken conversation, turns are allocated by a set of rules that exploit the real-time audibility of overlap, intonation, and gaze; adjacency pairs (question-answer, greeting-greeting) are enforced by the immediate expectation that the second part follow the first. In text chat, messages are composed in parallel and posted when the sender hits return; they arrive in the order the server receives them, not in the order they were begun. The result is that a reply frequently appears several lines after the message it addresses, separated from its target by unrelated turns from other participants. Adjacency, as a structural property of the transcript, is routinely disrupted. Multiple conversations run simultaneously in a single channel, their turns interleaved. Topics decay not because participants lose interest but because the thread of a topic is visually and sequentially broken by the arrival of competing material. Overlap, which in speech is fleeting and repaired, becomes a persistent feature of the written record.

Herring's methodological move is to treat the chat transcript itself as the analytic object. Because the interaction is text, every turn is preserved; the log is not a recording of the conversation but the conversation itself, and analysis proceeds by reading back through it. This has consequences she foregrounds. Coherence, in this medium, is not produced moment-to-moment by co-present interlocutors monitoring each other; it is reconstructed, by participants and by analysts alike, from a persistent written record in which the sequential cues of speech are absent or scrambled. Participants must do interpretive work to determine which message answers which, often relying on explicit addressing conventions, topical content, or quoted fragments to link a turn to its referent across several intervening lines. The transcript is both the site where the interaction occurs and the artifact from which its structure must be recovered.

Having established the extent of the incoherence, Herring turns to the question of why the medium nonetheless works. She offers two explanations. The first is adaptive: users develop conventions — naming the addressee, quoting, elliptical reference — that compensate for the loss of sequential adjacency. Competence in chat is, in part, competence in these repair strategies. The second is that the loosening of turn-taking constraints is not only tolerated but valued. Multiple overlapping threads allow a kind of multiparty interactivity impossible in speech, where only one person can hold the floor at a time. The disrupted structure enables play, parallel side-conversations, and a density of exchange that users experience as lively rather than chaotic. What conversation analysis would score as incoherence is, for participants, part of the appeal.

The survey frames CMC coherence as a genuinely different object from spoken coherence rather than a degraded version of it. The persistence of the written record, the collapse of adjacency, the interleaving of parallel threads, and the interpretive burden placed on any later reader become defining features of the medium rather than defects to be engineered away.
