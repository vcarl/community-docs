# Readers Are Not Free-Riders: Reading as a Form of Participation on Wikipedia

Antin, J., & Cheshire, C. (2010). Readers Are Not Free-Riders: Reading as a Form of Participation on Wikipedia. *Proceedings of CSCW 2010*, 127–130. https://doi.org/10.1145/1718918.1718942

## Abstract

The success of Wikipedia as a large-scale collaborative effort has spurred researchers to examine the motivations and behaviors of Wikipedia's participants. However, this research has tended to focus on active involvement rather than more common forms of participation such as reading. In this paper we argue that Wikipedia's readers should not all be characterized as free-riders — individuals who knowingly choose to take advantage of others' effort. The paper illustrates how readers provide a valuable service to Wikipedia, and draws on the notion of legitimate peripheral participation to argue that reading is a gateway activity through which newcomers learn about the project.

## AI Summary

Antin and Cheshire (2010) take aim at a rhetorical default that had settled over much of the early Wikipedia research literature: the framing of non-editing users as "free-riders." In economic usage, a free-rider is someone who benefits from a collective good without contributing to its production, and who, critically, understands the arrangement and chooses it. The authors' central move — compact but pointed, given the four-page scope of a CSCW note — is to argue that this framing misdescribes what Wikipedia's readers actually do and what they actually know, and that it obscures forms of value that readers both receive and supply.

The empirical backbone of the paper is a survey of 165 undergraduates about their use of Wikipedia. The instrument asks how often respondents read articles, how often they read Talk pages, how often they edit either, and administers a short operational-knowledge quiz covering Wikipedia's functional affordances, its policies and structures, and its conventions around authorship and audience. The distributions are stark. Essentially all respondents read Wikipedia and most do so multiple times per week, yet only a small minority have ever edited, and only a negligible fraction edit with any regularity. Talk pages are almost invisible to ordinary readers. Operational knowledge is low on average — respondents answer fewer than three of ten knowledge questions correctly — and it is unevenly distributed in ways the authors treat as diagnostic rather than incidental.

From these data the authors draw two linked arguments. The first is that the "free-rider" label depends on a knowing choice not to contribute, and the knowledge-quiz results suggest that most readers are not in a position to make that choice. They do not know that they can edit in the sense of having a working mental model of how editing is done, what the policy environment looks like, or what happens on Talk pages. Characterizing them as strategic non-contributors imputes a calculation they have not performed. The second argument is that reading itself is a productive activity with respect to the community. Readers constitute the audience whose attention justifies the editing labor of contributors; without them the encyclopedia's value as a public good collapses. They are also, by the authors' reading of the data, the population from which future editors are drawn — operational knowledge is most strongly associated with reading Talk pages rather than with editing per se, suggesting that exposure to the back-channel where editorial work is negotiated is where readers first acquire the competence a contribution would require.

To frame this, the authors invoke Lave and Wenger's notion of legitimate peripheral participation: newcomers to a community of practice begin at its edges, absorbing its norms and vocabulary through observation, and move inward as they acquire fluency. Reading on Wikipedia, on this account, is not the absence of participation but its earliest and most common form. It has value to the reader — as a route into the project's practices, and, the authors note, as a contributor to readers' own identity and self-understanding as participants in the project — and it has value to the community, which depends on an audience and on a pool from which editors can emerge.

The implication the authors draw is that community-design and research attention cannot be centered only on posting and editing behavior. A community whose metrics and moral vocabulary treat contribution as the only form of participation will misread its own membership, underinvest in what readers need, and mischaracterize as parasitic a population that is in fact supplying conditions of the project's viability. The note's scope is modest and its sample is a convenience one, but the conceptual reframing has outsized reach: it converts an economic pejorative into an empirical question and answers it, for this setting, against the pejorative.
