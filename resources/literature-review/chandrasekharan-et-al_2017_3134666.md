# You Can't Stay Here: The Efficacy of Reddit's 2015 Ban Examined Through Hate Speech

## Abstract
This study examines the effectiveness of Reddit's June 2015 ban of two prominent hate communities: r/fatpeoplehate and r/CoonTown. The authors analyzed whether banning these subreddits reduced hate speech on the platform overall. Using computational methods to measure hate speech across Reddit before and after the bans, the researchers found that the bans were effective: hate speech decreased significantly on the platform, and the "refugee" users who migrated to other subreddits did not bring significant increases in hate speech to their new communities.

## AI Summary
Chandrasekharan et al. (2017) conducted one of the most influential empirical studies of community-level banning in online platforms. The research examined Reddit's high-profile decision in June 2015 to ban two prominent hate communities: r/fatpeoplehate, which had approximately 150,000 subscribers and was dedicated to mocking and harassing overweight people, and r/CoonTown, a community centered on racist content. These bans were controversial at the time, with significant debate about whether removing these communities would actually reduce hate speech or simply scatter toxic users across the rest of the platform.

The central research question was direct and consequential: does banning hate communities actually reduce hate speech on a platform, or does it just displace toxic users elsewhere? Critics of community bans had long argued a "whack-a-mole" theory -- that banning a community is futile because its members will simply relocate to other communities and continue their behavior, potentially even radicalizing further in response to perceived persecution. This argument had been influential in platform policy discussions and was often cited as a reason for inaction.

The researchers used computational methods to measure hate speech across Reddit both before and after the bans. By tracking hate speech levels at a platform-wide scale, they could assess whether the total volume of hateful content changed or merely redistributed. They also specifically tracked the "refugee" users -- those who had been active in the banned communities and subsequently migrated to other subreddits -- to determine whether these users brought elevated levels of hate speech to their new communities.

The key finding was unambiguous: banning worked. Hate speech decreased significantly on Reddit as a whole following the community bans. The platform saw a measurable, meaningful reduction in hateful content rather than a simple redistribution. Furthermore, the refugee users who migrated to other subreddits did not bring significant increases in hate speech to those receiving communities. While some former members of the banned subreddits did continue to use Reddit, they did not replicate the same intensity or volume of hateful content in their new locations.

This finding directly challenged the whack-a-mole argument and provided some of the first rigorous empirical evidence that community-level bans can be effective tools for reducing harmful content on a platform. The paper demonstrated that the concentrated, reinforcing social dynamics within dedicated hate communities play a critical role in amplifying hate speech -- when that social infrastructure is dismantled, individual users tend to moderate their behavior, even without direct individual-level enforcement.

The study has been hugely influential in platform policy debates. It is frequently cited by researchers, journalists, and policy advocates as evidence supporting decisive action against hate communities. The paper helped shift the discourse from theoretical arguments about displacement to evidence-based analysis of outcomes, providing empirical grounding for platforms considering whether to ban communities that systematically violate their policies. It remains a foundational reference in the content moderation literature and has informed subsequent research on deplatforming and community-level enforcement actions.
