# Code of Conduct in Open Source Projects

**Authors:** Parastou Tourani, Bram Adams, Alexander Serebrenik
**Published:** 2017, IEEE 24th International Conference on Software Analysis, Evolution and Reengineering (SANER), pp. 24-33
**DOI:** 10.1109/SANER.2017.7884606
**Link:** https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7884606/

## Abstract

Open source projects rely on collaboration of members from all around the world using web technologies like GitHub and Gerrit. This mixture of people with a wide range of backgrounds including minorities like women, ethnic minorities, and people with disabilities may increase the risk of offensive and destroying behaviours in the community, potentially leading affected project members to leave towards a more welcoming and friendly environment. To counter these effects, open source projects increasingly are turning to codes of conduct, in an attempt to promote their expectations and standards of ethical behaviour. In this first of its kind empirical study of codes of conduct in open source software projects, we investigated the role, scope and influence of codes of conduct through a mixture of quantitative and qualitative analysis, supported by interviews with practitioners. We found that the top codes of conduct are adopted by hundreds to thousands of projects, while all of them share 5 common dimensions.

## AI Summary

This is the foundational empirical study of codes of conduct in open source software (OSS), the first to systematically examine CoCs in this context. The study uses a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative analysis of CoC adoption patterns, qualitative content analysis of CoC documents, and semi-structured interviews with OSS practitioners (maintainers and contributors).

For the quantitative component, the researchers surveyed the landscape of CoC adoption across open source projects on GitHub and other platforms, identifying which CoCs were most commonly adopted. They found that 11 distinct codes of conduct were in common use, with the Contributor Covenant being the most widely adopted by far, used by hundreds to thousands of projects.

The qualitative content analysis examined the text of major CoCs and identified 5 common dimensions shared across all of them: (1) promoting a welcoming and inclusive environment, (2) defining acceptable and unacceptable behaviors, (3) specifying enforcement mechanisms and consequences, (4) designating responsible parties for enforcement, and (5) establishing scope of application.

Through practitioner interviews, the researchers explored how CoCs function in practice. They found that enforcement is the most challenging aspect — many projects adopt a CoC but lack clear processes for handling violations. The interviews revealed tensions between those who view CoCs as necessary protections for vulnerable contributors and those who see them as unnecessary bureaucracy or threats to free expression in OSS culture.

Key findings include: CoC adoption is concentrated among larger, more established projects; enforcement mechanisms vary widely in specificity and formality; the Contributor Covenant's dominance means most projects share similar normative frameworks; and practitioners report that CoCs are most valuable as signaling devices — they communicate project values to potential contributors before they join.

The study's limitations include its focus on English-language projects and the difficulty of measuring behavioral change attributable to CoC adoption. The researchers note that adoption rates were relatively low at the time of study, though growing rapidly. This paper established the research agenda that subsequent studies (Li et al. 2021, Singh et al. 2021, Frluckaj & Howison 2024) would follow, making it a key reference point in the literature.
