# Censored, Suspended, Shadowbanned: User Interpretations of Content Moderation on Social Media Platforms

Myers West, S. (2018). Censored, Suspended, Shadowbanned: User Interpretations of Content Moderation on Social Media Platforms. *New Media & Society*, 20(11), 4366–4383. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444818773059

## Abstract

Social media platforms play an increasingly important civic role as platforms for discourse, where we discuss, debate, and share information. This article explores how users make sense of the content moderation systems social media platforms use to curate this discourse. Through a survey of users (n = 519) who have experienced content moderation, the author explores users' folk theories of how content moderation systems work, how they shape the affective relationship between users and platforms, and the steps users take to assert their agency by seeking redress. The author raises questions about what content moderation systems are designed to accomplish and concludes by conceptualizing an educational, rather than punitive, model for content moderation systems.

## AI Summary

Myers West (2018) studied how ordinary users on major commercial platforms understood moderation actions taken against their own accounts and content, working from a sample of 519 people who had experienced takedowns, suspensions, or suspected suppression. The recruitment approach — participants came through the "Online Censorship" project, which collected user-reported cases across Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Google+ — let her assemble a corpus of first-person accounts and associated survey responses from users whose knowledge of why they were moderated was typically partial or absent. Qualitative analysis of these accounts, combined with descriptive statistics from the survey, drove the paper's central move: treating users' explanations of moderation as data in their own right, rather than as noise to be corrected against platform ground truth.

The central empirical finding was that users, confronted with enforcement actions whose reasoning they could not see, produced elaborate and recurring explanatory patterns. Myers West identified several clusters of these folk theories. One cluster personified the moderation apparatus, attributing intent, mood, or bias to "the algorithm" or to a presumed human reviewer who had misread the content. A second cluster leaned toward conspiracy-style explanations: platforms were thought to be acting on behalf of political factions, advertisers, foreign governments, or ideological opponents, with enforcement patterns read as evidence of deliberate targeting. A third cluster challenged the authority of the platform directly, framing the action as illegitimate overreach against speech that users believed they had a right to post. These were not idiosyncratic responses; they were patterned ways of generating a plausible account when the platform itself offered none.

The paper traced how these interpretations emerged from the structural features of contemporary moderation. Notifications, when they arrived at all, typically cited broad community standards without specifying which provision had been violated or how the specific content matched it. Appeals channels were often absent, slow, or opaque, and users frequently could not confirm whether their account had in fact been penalized — the category of "shadowbanning," covered extensively in the article, captured precisely this ambiguity, where reach appeared to collapse without any formal notice. In the absence of explanation, users reasoned from the limited signals available: the identity of the content, the timing of the action, their sense of who else was or was not being moderated, and their prior beliefs about the platform's politics. Folk theories were the predictable output of this reasoning environment.

Myers West documented the behavioral consequences of these interpretations. Users reported altering what they posted, self-censoring across topics they now treated as risky, reducing engagement with the platform, or migrating activity elsewhere. The chilling effect she described was not a reaction to any communicated rule; it was a reaction to the users' own constructed model of what the rule might be. Because that model was built from inference under uncertainty, it tended to overshoot — users avoided a broader range of expression than the platform's actual policies would have required — and it tended to corrode the affective relationship with the platform, generating resentment, distrust, and a sense that one was operating under a hidden and arbitrary regime.

The paper closed by arguing that the prevailing punitive architecture — detect, remove, notify minimally, defend the outcome — was poorly suited to the civic role platforms had come to occupy. Myers West proposed an educational alternative in which enforcement actions were accompanied by specific, contextual explanations of which rule had been applied and why, and in which the moderation interface was designed to teach users the norms of the space rather than merely to sanction them. The argument rested on the evidence the study had assembled: when users were left to infer the rules from enforcement patterns, they inferred incorrectly, reacted self-protectively, and lost trust in the system doing the enforcing.
