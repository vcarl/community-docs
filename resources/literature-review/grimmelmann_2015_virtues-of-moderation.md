# The Virtues of Moderation

Grimmelmann, J. (2015). The Virtues of Moderation. *Yale Journal of Law & Technology*, 17, 42–109.

## Abstract

*The following is reconstructed from the published abstract as distributed through the Yale Journal of Law & Technology and the Cornell Law Scholarship repository; the phrasing paraphrases the author's framing where verbatim text could not be confirmed.*

The article opens with a contrast between the Los Angeles Times "wikitorial" — a 2005 open-editing experiment that collapsed into vandalism within a weekend — and Wikipedia, which has sustained large-scale open contribution for more than a decade. The difference, Grimmelmann argues, is moderation: the governance mechanisms that structure participation in a community to facilitate cooperation and prevent abuse. Moderators promote posts or hide them, honor posters or shame them, recruit users or ban them. The article offers a novel taxonomy of moderation in online communities, breaking down the basic verbs of moderation — exclusion, pricing, organizing, and norm-setting — and showing how they help communities walk the tightrope between the chaos of too much freedom and the sterility of too much control. The account draws on commons scholarship and is positioned to inform policy debates about the regulation of online communities.

## AI Summary

Grimmelmann (2015) sets out to provide what the legal literature on online communities had, to that point, largely lacked: a systematic vocabulary for describing how those communities are actually governed. His central move is definitional. Moderation, in his account, is not a peripheral janitorial function — removing spam, banning trolls — but the set of governance mechanisms that structure participation in a community to facilitate cooperation and prevent abuse. Under this definition, a community without moderation is not a purer or freer community; it is a community whose governance is simply invisible and usually failing. The working examples that open the article, the Los Angeles Times wikitorial and Wikipedia, differ not in whether they are moderated but in whether the moderation they have is sufficient to sustain cooperation at the scale they invite.

The taxonomy the article develops has four elements, which Grimmelmann presents as the basic verbs available to anyone who would shape a community. **Exclusion** controls who may participate at all: bans, membership requirements, invite gates, and access controls of every kind. **Pricing** controls the cost of participation, where cost is construed broadly to include money, effort, delay, reputation risk, and attention — karma thresholds, posting limits, waiting periods, and paywalls are all pricing mechanisms in this sense. **Organizing** controls how content is arranged and surfaced: sorting, ranking, tagging, threading, pinning, collapsing, and search. **Norm-setting** shapes what participants treat as appropriate conduct, through rules, examples, rituals, public praise and public shaming, and the modeling behavior of visible members. Grimmelmann argues that these four categories exhaust the levers a moderator actually has. Any specific moderation practice — a reputation score, a subreddit rule, a voting system, a karma decay function, a "flair" convention — can be decomposed into some combination of excluding, pricing, organizing, and norm-setting. The taxonomy is meant to be complete in this sense, not merely suggestive.

A second strand of the argument is that the availability of these verbs is not uniform across platforms. Which techniques a community can use, and how cheaply, depends on the technical and legal structure of the environment in which it operates. A platform that makes identity cheap and revocable makes exclusion ineffective; a platform that does not expose ranking controls denies its communities organizing as a lever; a platform whose terms and applicable law forbid certain pricing moves (charging for access, demanding real-name verification) removes them from the menu. Grimmelmann treats this as the reason moderation cannot be discussed in the abstract. The same community problem — say, a flood of low-quality contributions — has different feasible solutions depending on what the underlying system will let moderators do. Design choices at the platform layer determine the governance choices available at the community layer.

From this follows the article's characteristic stance toward the design of participation. Because the four verbs are the complete set, and because their availability is shaped by the substrate, the question for any community is not whether to moderate but which mixture of exclusion, pricing, organizing, and norm-setting best fits its goals, its scale, and the constraints imposed on it from below. Communities that underuse one verb typically overuse another; communities that cannot use a given verb at all must compensate with the remaining three. Grimmelmann develops these dynamics through short case treatments — Wikipedia, Metafilter, Slashdot, eBay, Usenet, and others — each chosen to illustrate a different combination of levers responding to a different structural environment.

The article's broader claim, which the taxonomy serves, is that moderation is constitutive of community rather than supplemental to it. Cooperation at scale among strangers does not arise on its own and is not preserved by good intentions; it is produced by the continuous operation of the four verbs, backed by whatever technical and normative infrastructure the community has assembled. To describe a functioning online community is, on Grimmelmann's account, already to describe a moderation regime, whether or not its participants recognize it as one.
