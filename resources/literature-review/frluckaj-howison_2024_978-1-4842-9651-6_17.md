# Codes of Conduct in Open Source

**Authors:** Hana Frluckaj, James Howison
**Published:** 2024, in Damian, D., Blincoe, K., Ford, D., Serebrenik, A., Masood, Z. (eds) Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion in Software Engineering. Apress, Berkeley, CA
**DOI:** 10.1007/978-1-4842-9651-6_17
**Link:** https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4842-9651-6_17

## Abstract

Codes of conduct (CoCs) have become a hot topic in open source software as contributors and projects increasingly discuss their messaging, presence, and importance. This chapter aims to provide a holistic overview of CoCs and research on them. We first provide the history, context, and controversies surrounding CoCs and demonstrate why CoCs are an important document and tool for OSS projects. We then showcase findings from the literature on CoCs and finally identify open research questions and call for their exploration.

## AI Summary

This book chapter from the "Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion in Software Engineering" volume provides both a literature review of CoC research and original empirical findings from two ongoing studies: one of trans, non-binary, and genderqueer contributors to OSS, and another of OSS maintainers.

The chapter begins with historical context, tracing the introduction of CoCs in OSS to 2014 when Coraline Ada Ehmke created the Contributor Covenant following reports of sexualized language, assaults at events, and a general lack of governance in OSS communities. The authors document the cultural war that followed: OSS traditionalists viewed CoCs as threats to free speech and open culture, while advocates — particularly women and other underrepresented groups — saw them as necessary protections. The authors note that underrepresented groups disproportionately bear the labor of advocacy for CoCs and other community-oriented tasks.

The chapter then reviews the structure of major CoCs (Contributor Covenant, Mozilla's Guidelines, Google's CoC, Python CoC, Django CoC, Rust CoC), identifying common structural elements: pledges, behavioral standards, enforcement responsibilities, and enforcement guidelines (typically escalating from warnings to temporary bans to permanent bans).

The original empirical contribution draws from interviews with 21 trans contributors and 21 OSS maintainers. For trans contributors, the key finding is that CoC presence serves as a "screening signal" during what the researchers call "reconnaissance work" — the process of evaluating a project's safety before deciding to contribute. Participants described using CoC presence as a rough heuristic for whether a community would be trans-friendly. However, participants consistently emphasized that enforcement matters more than presence: observing how community leadership responds to transphobic behavior was a stronger indicator of safety than whether a CoC document existed.

Maintainers interviewed reported awareness of the CoC's signaling function and described using it to communicate community values. Some maintainers spoke passionately about proactive inclusivity; others acknowledged the potentially performative nature of CoCs, with one participant comparing unenforced CoCs to "greenwashing."

The chapter identifies critical open research questions: How does the lack of a designated enforcement contact affect CoC effectiveness? How widespread is the deletion of negative interactions, and what are the long-term implications? How can projects best facilitate community deliberation on CoC content? What is the optimal balance between nurturing enforcement (educating infringers) and punitive enforcement (swift consequences)?

The key takeaway is that CoCs exist on a spectrum from genuine governance instruments to performative signals, and the difference lies entirely in enforcement. The authors recommend community consensus in CoC creation, visible and consistent enforcement, and preserving (anonymized) records of moderation decisions as learning resources.
