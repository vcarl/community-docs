Nagin synthesizes several decades of research on criminal deterrence, organizing the evidence around the classical distinction between the certainty, severity, and celerity of punishment. He argues that the body of modern evidence consistently supports a deterrent effect of the certainty of apprehension, while evidence for the marginal deterrent effect of increasing already-substantial sentence severity is weak and inconsistent. He draws out the policy implications — in particular that lengthy prison sentences and mandatory minimums cannot be justified on deterrence grounds — and identifies open research problems, including how policing practices actually shape potential offenders' perceptions of the probability of being caught and how the experience of punishment interacts with the threat of punishment.

## AI Summary

Nagin (2013) takes stock of roughly four decades of research on criminal deterrence and argues that the accumulated evidence, read carefully, overturns a long-standing popular and political intuition: that making sanctions more severe is the main lever for deterring crime. The review organizes its argument around the three classical dimensions of punishment — certainty, severity, and celerity — and concludes that the weight of credible evidence concentrates on only one of them. Increases in the certainty of apprehension show consistent deterrent effects across study designs and settings; increases in severity, once sanctions are already non-trivial, do not. Celerity has barely been studied in a way that permits strong inference and is set aside.

A central move of the essay is to insist that "certainty" in the criminological literature is not really about the objective probability of punishment in some abstract statistical sense; it is about the probability of apprehension, and it operates through what potential offenders believe that probability to be. Nagin reads the most informative recent work — studies of policing deployments, hot-spots experiments, and quasi-experimental shifts in enforcement visibility — as showing that what changes behavior is the visible presence and activity of enforcement, which is what updates people's beliefs about their chances of being caught. The deterrent channel, in other words, runs through perception. An enforcement regime that is objectively strict but invisible, or that is strict on paper but perceived to be lax, will not produce the behavioral response that its formal parameters would predict. Conversely, visible, patterned, responsive enforcement can produce a deterrent signal out of proportion to its objective sanction levels.

This reframing has consequences for how Nagin reads the severity literature. Studies of long sentences, three-strikes laws, and mandatory minimums generally fail to find marginal deterrent effects of the kind their proponents assert. Nagin's explanation is consistent with the perceptual account: would-be offenders are typically poorly informed about the back end of the sentencing schedule, discount distant consequences heavily, and in any case make decisions under conditions where the salient question is whether they will be caught at all, not what happens after conviction. Severity is doing less work than legal theorists and policymakers have assumed, partly because the channel through which it would have to operate — accurate perception of sentence lengths — is itself weak.

The review also draws out a broader point about the population through which deterrence operates. Deterrence is not principally a matter of what happens to a given offender after they are sanctioned; it is a matter of what bystanders and would-be offenders learn from watching what happens to others, and from the ambient visibility of enforcement in their environment. The behavioral effect of a sanction on the sanctioned individual is a separate — and, Nagin notes, potentially criminogenic — question. The deterrent payoff, to the extent there is one, accrues through the observing population as they revise their beliefs about the likelihood and character of enforcement. This distinction matters for policy because it locates the relevant variable not in how harshly a legal system punishes those it catches but in how legibly and consistently enforcement is experienced by those it might catch next.

A further thread in the essay is the role of perceived legitimacy. Nagin notes evidence that compliance responds not only to the perceived probability of sanction but to whether enforcement is experienced as fair and procedurally proper. Enforcement that is visibly patterned and rule-governed generates compliance through a different channel than raw fear of sanction: it sustains the belief that the authority doing the enforcing has the standing to do it. Strategies that maximize apparent certainty through tactics perceived as arbitrary or discriminatory can undercut the very perceptual conditions on which deterrence depends. Nagin treats legitimacy and perceived certainty as intertwined inputs to the same behavioral outcome rather than as separate literatures, and he flags the interaction as an area where further causal work is needed.
