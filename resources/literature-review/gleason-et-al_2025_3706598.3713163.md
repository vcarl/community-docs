# In Suspense About Suspensions? How Varying the Type and Length of Consequence Affects User Behavior

## Abstract

It is common for digital platforms to issue consequences for behaviors that violate Community Standards policies. However, there is limited evidence about the relative effectiveness of consequences, particularly lengths of temporary suspensions. To investigate this, we report on a large-scale quasi-experiment at Meta examining how the type (warning, restriction, and suspension) and length of consequence (ranging from 1 to 30 days) impact user behavior on Facebook. Applying a regression discontinuity design, we find that suspensions (temporarily losing all access to the platform) are the most effective consequence for reducing recidivism, while warnings (the lightest consequence) are the least effective. We also find that longer suspensions are more effective than shorter suspensions at reducing violations, though the marginal benefit of each additional day decreases as length increases. Our findings suggest that the design of consequences matters for effectiveness, and we discuss implications for designing effective enforcement systems.

Gleason, Leavitt, and Daly (2025) report on a large-scale quasi-experiment conducted at Meta examining how different types and lengths of enforcement consequences affect user behavior on Facebook. This study is notable as a rare piece of industry research with access to platform-scale data, providing direct empirical evidence on the graduated enforcement approach that many platforms employ.

The study compared three types of consequences issued to users who violated Facebook's Community Standards: warnings (the lightest consequence, informing the user of the violation), restrictions (limiting the user's ability to use certain platform features), and suspensions (temporarily revoking all access to the platform). The length of consequences varied from 1 to 30 days, allowing the researchers to examine not only which type of consequence was most effective but also how the duration of enforcement affected outcomes.

The methodological approach employed a regression discontinuity design, a quasi-experimental technique that exploits natural thresholds in how consequences are assigned to estimate causal effects. This design is well-suited to platform enforcement contexts where randomized experiments may be ethically or practically difficult to conduct, and it provides stronger causal inference than purely observational approaches.

The key findings are threefold. First, suspensions — where users temporarily lose all access to the platform — are the most effective consequence for reducing recidivism (future violations). Warnings, the lightest form of consequence, are the least effective. Restrictions fall between the two. This establishes a clear hierarchy of enforcement effectiveness tied to the severity of the consequence.

Second, longer suspensions are more effective than shorter suspensions at reducing future violations. However, the relationship is not linear: the marginal benefit of each additional day of suspension decreases as the suspension length increases. This means that while extending a suspension from 1 day to 7 days produces a substantial improvement in behavior change, extending from 23 days to 30 days yields a much smaller additional benefit. This finding of diminishing marginal returns has important practical implications for calibrating enforcement policies.

Third, the overall conclusion is that the design of consequences matters significantly for their effectiveness at changing user behavior. This is not a trivial finding, as many platforms have developed their enforcement escalation ladders based on intuition, industry convention, or legal requirements rather than empirical evidence of what actually reduces future violations.

The practical implications of this research are substantial for platform governance and online community management. The findings provide direct evidence supporting a graduated enforcement approach where consequences escalate in severity, while also suggesting that there are practical limits to the effectiveness of longer suspensions. Community managers and platform designers can use these findings to calibrate their enforcement systems more effectively, balancing the goal of deterring future violations against the diminishing returns of increasingly severe punishments. This research bridges the gap between platform-scale enforcement policy and empirical evidence of behavioral outcomes.
