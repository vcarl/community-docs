# Sharing the Small Moments: Ephemeral Social Interaction on Snapchat

Bayer, J. B., Ellison, N. B., Schoenebeck, S. Y., & Falk, E. B. (2016). Sharing the Small Moments: Ephemeral Social Interaction on Snapchat. *Information, Communication & Society*, 19(7), 956–977. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2015.1084349

## Abstract

Ephemeral social media, platforms that display shared content for a limited period of time, have become a prominent component of the social ecosystem. We draw on experience sampling data collected over two weeks (Study 1; N=154) and in-depth interview data from a subsample of participants (Study 2; N=28) to understand college students' social and emotional experiences on Snapchat, a popular ephemeral mobile platform. Our quantitative data demonstrated that Snapchat interactions were perceived as more enjoyable – and associated with more positive mood – than other communication technologies (i.e., calling, texting, emailing, Facebook). However, Snapchat interactions were also associated with lower social support than other channels. Our qualitative data highlighted aspects of Snapchat use that may facilitate positive affect (but not social support), including sharing mundane experiences with close ties and reduced self-presentational concerns. In addition, users compared Snapchat to face-to-face interaction and reported attending to Snapchat content more closely than archived content, which may contribute to increased emotional rewards. Interestingly, participants did not see the application as a platform for sharing or viewing photos; rather, Snapchat was viewed as a lightweight channel for sharing spontaneous experiences with close ties. Together, these two studies contribute to our evolving understanding of ephemeral social media and their role in social relationships.

## AI Summary

Bayer, Ellison, Schoenebeck, and Falk (2016) study what happens to social interaction when the default expectation of online communication — that posts, photos, and messages persist and accumulate — is removed. Snapchat, at the time of the study, automatically deleted received content after a short viewing window, and the authors treat this property as a defining design feature rather than a novelty. By comparing Snapchat use to other communication channels within the same participants' daily lives, they can ask a concrete empirical question: what changes, in the texture and consequences of everyday communication, when the medium discards its own record?

The research design combines an experience sampling study of 154 college students over two weeks with follow-up interviews of a 28-person subsample. Experience sampling is well suited to this question because it samples interactions in situ rather than relying on retrospective summaries of "typical" use; participants report on specific recent exchanges, and the aggregated picture reflects the distribution of actual moments rather than how users frame their habits after the fact. The interviews then let participants articulate, in their own terms, how Snapchat fits into a broader repertoire of channels. The combination is what gives the paper its weight: a pattern that appears both in moment-level ratings and in reflective talk is harder to dismiss as an artifact of either method.

The quantitative picture is striking in its asymmetry. Snapchat interactions registered as more enjoyable and more positively toned than exchanges over calling, texting, email, or Facebook — and at the same time as delivering less social support. The two findings pull in different directions and force a more careful account of what the platform is actually doing. It is not simply a more "satisfying" medium; it is a medium whose satisfactions are concentrated in affective, light-touch exchange rather than in the provision of help, advice, or serious emotional work. Participants did not look to Snapchat when they needed to be heard about something difficult; they used it for a different register of contact entirely.

The interview data clarify what that register is. Users described Snapchat as a place for sharing mundane, in-the-moment experiences with close ties — what might otherwise have remained unshared because it was not worth the effort of a properly framed post. Self-presentational concerns were notably relaxed. On platforms where content persists, users described an awareness of an extended, partly imagined audience and a corresponding pressure to produce messages that would hold up under later review. That pressure receded on Snapchat. Because the content would not be there tomorrow, it did not need to be worth keeping; it could be rough, unflattering, silly, or trivial without reputational cost. Participants described, in effect, permission to send things they would not otherwise send.

The authors also note that recipients reported attending more closely to Snapchat content than to archived content, a small but consequential observation. When a message cannot be returned to, the viewer must be present for it in a way that scrolling back through an archive does not require. Attention and transience are linked.

Taken together, the studies describe a communication environment shaped by a single design choice about retention. Removing the record does not merely change what the platform stores; it changes what participants feel able to say, to whom, and about what. The comparison set — calling, texting, email, Facebook — makes the contrast explicit rather than theoretical. Everyday, unserious, affectively warm contact with close ties thrives where the audience is small and the record is short. Where content persists and the audience is ambient, the same communicative register becomes costly to produce, and users produce less of it.
