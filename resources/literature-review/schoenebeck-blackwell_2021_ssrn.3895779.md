# Reimagining Social Media Governance: Harm, Accountability, and Repair

## Abstract

The piece addresses persistent challenges in managing online communities, noting that social media platforms continue to both cause and facilitate harm. The authors contend that while many platform-related problems reflect broader societal inequities, companies bear responsibility for addressing the consequences they enable. Current governance practices emphasize content and account removal based on policy violations but neglect those experiencing negative effects. The researchers argue this approach mirrors punitive criminal justice frameworks rather than fostering systemic improvement through accountability mechanisms and restorative practices. Drawing on justice-centered perspectives, they propose fundamental adjustments to how social media companies should acknowledge and remedy harms.

## AI Summary

Schoenebeck and Blackwell (2021) present a theoretical framework for rethinking social media governance through a restorative justice lens. Published in the Yale Journal of Law & Technology, this paper represents a conceptual contribution rather than an empirical study, arguing that the current paradigm of platform enforcement is fundamentally misaligned with the goal of creating healthier online communities.

The central argument is that current platform moderation practices -- focused on content removal, warnings, suspensions, and bans -- mirror punitive criminal justice frameworks. Under this model, the primary question platforms ask is whether a specific piece of content or behavior violated a rule, and the primary response is punishment of the violator. The authors identify several problems with this approach.

First, current moderation focuses overwhelmingly on punishing violators but neglects the victims and targets of harm. When someone experiences harassment, hate speech, or other forms of online abuse, the platform's response is directed at the perpetrator (removing content, issuing warnings or bans) rather than at supporting the person who was harmed. This leaves targets of abuse without meaningful recourse, acknowledgment, or repair.

Second, the authors argue that platforms should adopt accountability mechanisms where violators are asked to acknowledge the harm they have caused. Rather than simply removing content and moving on, a governance framework centered on accountability would require those who cause harm to understand and take responsibility for its impact. This represents a fundamental shift from rule enforcement toward behavioral change and community healing.

Third, Schoenebeck and Blackwell propose that restorative practices such as mediation and repair could be more effective than purely punitive approaches. Drawing on restorative justice principles from criminal justice reform, they envision processes where those who cause harm, those who experience harm, and the broader community can engage in structured dialogue aimed at understanding, accountability, and restoration. This approach prioritizes repairing relationships and community trust over punishment alone.

Fourth, the paper addresses how platform governance reflects and amplifies existing societal inequities. The authors argue that content moderation policies and their enforcement are not neutral -- they disproportionately affect marginalized communities and often fail to account for the power dynamics and structural inequalities that shape online interactions. A justice-centered approach to governance would need to grapple with these inequities explicitly.

The paper proposes a fundamental reorientation of platform governance from the question "did this content violate a rule?" to "who was harmed and how can it be repaired?" This shift in framing has significant implications for how platforms design their moderation systems, train their moderators, and engage with their communities.

This work is influential in the growing field of restorative justice approaches to online governance. It represents a philosophical counterpoint to the enforcement-focused papers that dominate content moderation research, challenging the assumption that better enforcement alone can solve the problems of online harm. By centering the experiences of those who are harmed and proposing mechanisms for accountability and repair, Schoenebeck and Blackwell offer a vision of platform governance that goes beyond policing content to building healthier communities.
