# Online Moderation in Competitive Action Games: How Intervention Affects Player Behaviors

**Authors:** Zhuofang Li, Rafal Kocielnik, Mitchell Linegar, D. Sambrano, Fereshteh Soltani, Min Kim, Nabiha Naqvie, Grant Cahill, Anima Anandkumar, R. Michael Alvarez
**Published:** 2024, arXiv preprint
**arXiv:** 2411.01057
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01057

## Abstract

Online competitive action games have flourished as a space for entertainment and social connections, yet they face challenges from a small percentage of players engaging in disruptive behaviors. This study delves into the under-explored realm of understanding the effects of moderation on player behavior within online gaming on an example of a popular title - Call of Duty®: Modern Warfare®II. We employ a quasi-experimental design and causal inference techniques to examine the impact of moderation in a real-world industry-scale moderation system. We further delve into novel aspects around the impact of delayed moderation, as well as the severity of applied punishment. We examine these effects on a set of four disruptive behaviors including cheating, offensive user name, chat, and voice. Our findings uncover the dual impact moderation has on reducing disruptive behavior and discouraging disruptive players from participating. We further uncover differences in the effectiveness of quick and delayed moderation and the varying severity of punishment. Our examination of real-world gaming interactions sets a precedent in understanding the effectiveness of moderation and its impact on player behavior. Our insights offer actionable suggestions for the most promising avenues for improving real-world moderation practices, as well as the heterogeneous impact moderation has on indifferent players.

## AI Summary

This study provides rare causal evidence on the effectiveness of content moderation by leveraging data from an industry-scale moderation system in a major online game — Call of Duty: Modern Warfare II. Unlike most moderation research that relies on observational data, this study uses quasi-experimental methods and causal inference techniques to isolate the actual effect of moderation interventions on player behavior.

The methodology employs a quasi-experimental design taking advantage of natural variation in when and how moderation is applied to different players. By comparing the behavior trajectories of players who received moderation at different times and with different severity levels, the researchers can use causal inference techniques (likely difference-in-differences or instrumental variable approaches) to estimate the causal effect of moderation, controlling for selection effects (e.g., more disruptive players being more likely to be moderated).

The study examines four distinct categories of disruptive behavior: cheating (using unauthorized software or exploits), offensive usernames (names containing slurs or inappropriate content), chat violations (text-based harassment or hate speech), and voice violations (verbal harassment through voice chat). This granularity is important because different types of violations may respond differently to moderation.

The key findings reveal a dual impact of moderation. First, moderation reduces disruptive behavior among moderated players — those who receive penalties are less likely to engage in future violations. Second, moderation discourages disruptive players from participating at all — some players stop playing after being penalized, effectively removing themselves from the community. Both effects contribute to a less disruptive environment, but through different mechanisms (behavior change vs. attrition).

The study also examines two critical dimensions of moderation effectiveness: timing and severity. Quick moderation (penalties applied shortly after violations) was more effective at reducing future violations than delayed moderation. This echoes the PNAS finding (Schneider & Rizoiu 2023) about content half-lives — the sooner moderation occurs, the greater its impact. Regarding severity, the researchers found varying effectiveness across punishment levels, suggesting that the relationship between punishment severity and behavioral change is not linear.

An important nuance is the study's examination of "heterogeneous effects" on different player types. Moderation affects disruptive players differently depending on their patterns — some may reform their behavior, while others simply leave. The study also considers the impact on "indifferent players" — those who are neither particularly disruptive nor particularly well-behaved — finding that the overall moderation environment affects their behavior as well.

The conclusions offer actionable recommendations for moderation practice: prioritize swift moderation over delayed action, calibrate punishment severity to the type and frequency of violation, and consider that moderation has system-wide effects beyond the individual being punished. The study is valuable because it provides real-world causal evidence at industrial scale, making its findings more credible and generalizable than lab experiments or observational studies.
