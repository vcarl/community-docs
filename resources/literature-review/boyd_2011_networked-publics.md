# Social Network Sites as Networked Publics: Affordances, Dynamics, and Implications

boyd, d. (2010/2011). Social Network Sites as Networked Publics: Affordances, Dynamics, and Implications. In Z. Papacharissi (Ed.), *A Networked Self: Identity, Community, and Culture on Social Network Sites* (pp. 39–58). Routledge.

## Abstract

*This chapter does not carry a separate publisher abstract. The following is drawn verbatim from the opening paragraphs of the author's preprint on danah.org, which function as a summary of the chapter's argument.*

Social network sites have gained tremendous traction recently as a popular online hangout space for both youth and adults. People flock to them to socialize with their friends and acquaintances, to share information with interested others, and to see and be seen. In making sense of the practices that unfold on and through these sites, boyd comes to understand social network sites as a genre of "networked publics." Networked publics are publics that are restructured by networked technologies. As such, they are simultaneously (1) the space constructed through networked technologies and (2) the imagined collective that emerges as a result of the intersection of people, technology, and practice. Networked publics serve many of the same functions as other types of publics — they allow people to gather for social, cultural, and civic purposes and they help people connect with a world beyond their close friends and family. While networked publics share much in common with other types of publics, the ways in which technology structures them introduces distinct affordances that shape how people engage with these environments. The properties of bits — as distinct from atoms — introduce new possibilities for interaction. As a result, new dynamics emerge that shape participation. The chapter maps out the architecture of networked publics, beginning with the bits-based nature of digital environments and moving through the affordances of networked publics and the common dynamics those affordances produce.

## AI Summary

boyd (2010/2011) develops a conceptual vocabulary for analyzing social network sites by treating them as a genre of "networked publics" — publics that are at once a space constructed through networked technologies and the imagined collective that emerges from the intersection of people, technology, and practice. The chapter does not argue that technology determines behavior; it argues that the architecture of a given environment configures engagement, and that the architecture of networked publics differs from older public forms in ways that can be specified. That specification is the central analytical contribution.

The specification rests on a distinction between bits and atoms. Physical publics are composed of atoms and inherit the constraints atoms impose: co-presence, ephemerality, the boundedness of any given room. Digital publics are composed of bits, which behave differently. boyd identifies four properties of bit-based content that operate as structural affordances of networked publics. *Persistence*: online expressions are automatically recorded and archived, inverting the default in unmediated settings where speech is ephemeral. *Replicability*: content made of bits can be duplicated, and the copy is indistinguishable from the original. *Scalability*: the potential visibility of content in networked publics is great, though the chapter is careful to note that scalability is a possibility, not a guarantee, and that what actually scales is often not what the speaker wanted scaled. *Searchability*: content can be retrieved through search, making people and utterances findable in ways spoken speech never was. These four affordances are intertwined, but each contributes something distinct to the environment's structure.

From these affordances, boyd derives three dynamics that participants must contend with. The first is invisible audiences: in unmediated settings a speaker can see who is present, but in networked publics the audience includes lurkers, later readers, archive browsers, and anyone a search can reach. The second is collapsed contexts: the spatial, social, and temporal boundaries that normally let people maintain distinct performances for distinct audiences fail, so one utterance is received by people who would otherwise have been addressed separately and differently. The third is the blurring of public and private: once control over context is lost, the binary between the two becomes harder to maintain and has to be renegotiated through strategy rather than assumed as a setting of the environment.

The chapter's treatment of the audience problem is especially consequential. Because persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability operate together, a contribution that feels like a message to a familiar handful of Friends is, structurally, a broadcast. It can be read later, by someone the speaker did not anticipate, arriving via a search the speaker did not run, in a context the speaker cannot see. Participants cope by imagining an audience — typically their Friends list, or a subset of it — and calibrating self-presentation to that imagined group. The imagined audience lets people act at all in an environment where the actual audience is unknowable, but it systematically understates reach. What feels interpersonal is in fact recorded, copyable, potentially amplifiable, and retrievable later by strangers. The gap between perceived audience and actual audience is not a user error; it is produced by the architecture and is a general condition of participation.

boyd situates the analysis against earlier media theory, particularly Meyrowitz on broadcast media, to argue that the dynamics of networked publics are not entirely new but are newly general. Professional performers have always negotiated invisible audiences and collapsed contexts; what networked publics do is extend those conditions to ordinary people engaged in what feels like ordinary conversation. Scalability, the chapter notes, also does not evenly democratize — it tends to reproduce existing social and political stratification, amplifying what the collective chooses to amplify rather than what any individual wishes to see spread. The architecture is a structural force, but it operates through, not around, the social world it restructures.
