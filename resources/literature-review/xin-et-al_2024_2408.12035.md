# Let Community Rules Be Reflected in Online Content Moderation

**Authors:** Wangjiaxuan Xin, Kanlun Wang, Zhe Fu, Lina Zhou
**Published:** 2024, arXiv preprint
**arXiv:** 2408.12035
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12035

## Abstract

Content moderation is a widely used strategy to prevent the dissemination of irregular information on social media platforms. Despite extensive research on developing automated models to support decision-making in content moderation, there remains a notable scarcity of studies that integrate the rules of online communities into content moderation. This study addresses this gap by proposing a community rule-based content moderation framework that directly integrates community rules into the moderation of user-generated content. Our experiment results with datasets collected from two domains demonstrate the superior performance of models based on the framework to baseline models across all evaluation metrics. In particular, incorporating community rules substantially enhances model performance in content moderation. The findings of this research have significant research and practical implications for improving the effectiveness and generalizability of content moderation models in online communities.

## AI Summary

This study addresses a fundamental gap in automated content moderation research: most existing models are trained on generic toxicity/harm datasets and do not account for the specific rules and norms of individual communities. Different online communities have different standards — what is acceptable in a casual gaming forum may violate norms in a professional development community. The researchers propose a framework for integrating community-specific rules directly into automated content moderation systems.

The methodology involves developing a community rule-based content moderation framework and testing it experimentally. The framework works by encoding a community's specific rules (e.g., rules from subreddit sidebars, community guidelines, or codes of conduct) into a format that can be provided as context to machine learning models when they evaluate user-generated content. Rather than simply classifying content as "toxic" or "not toxic" based on general standards, the framework allows models to assess content against the particular rules of the community where it was posted.

The researchers collected datasets from two distinct online domains (the specific domains are not named in the abstract but represent communities with meaningfully different content norms). They then compared the performance of their community-rule-aware models against baseline models that lack community context. The evaluation used standard metrics for classification tasks (precision, recall, F1 score, and accuracy).

The key finding is that incorporating community rules substantially enhances model performance across all evaluation metrics. Models that were aware of community-specific rules made significantly better moderation decisions than models relying solely on general content guidelines. This improvement was consistent across both domains studied, suggesting the finding generalizes beyond a single community type.

The implications are significant for both research and practice. For research, the study demonstrates that content moderation cannot be treated as a context-free classification problem — community context matters. For practice, the framework provides a pathway for platforms to offer more nuanced, community-appropriate moderation rather than applying one-size-fits-all content standards. This is particularly relevant for platforms like Reddit, Discord, and GitHub that host many sub-communities with distinct cultures and norms.

The study connects directly to the broader literature on code of conduct effectiveness: it provides technical evidence that formalizing community rules — and integrating them into moderation systems — produces measurably better outcomes. This supports the argument from governance researchers (Li et al. 2021, Frluckaj & Howison 2024) that the content and specificity of community rules matter, not just their existence. When rules are clear enough to be operationalized in automated systems, moderation becomes more consistent and more aligned with community expectations.
