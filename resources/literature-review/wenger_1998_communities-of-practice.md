# Communities of Practice

Wenger, E. (1998). *Communities of Practice: Learning, Meaning, and Identity*. Cambridge University Press.

## Abstract

This book presents a theory of learning that starts with the assumption that engagement in social practice is the fundamental process by which we get to know what we know and by which we become who we are. The primary unit of analysis of this process is neither the individual nor social institutions, but the informal "communities of practice" that people form as they pursue shared enterprises over time. To give a social account of learning, the theory explores in a systematic way the intersection of issues of community, social practice, meaning, and identity, resulting in a broad conceptual framework for thinking about learning as a process of social participation.

## AI Summary

Wenger (1998) develops a social theory of learning in which what people come to know, and who they come to be, are inseparable from the communities of practice through which their participation is organized. The book extends a line of work Wenger had previously pursued with Jean Lave on legitimate peripheral participation, but it is not a continuation of the apprenticeship argument. It is a restatement of the underlying theory on a wider footing, reworked to cover forms of participation that apprenticeship-centered accounts had either treated as marginal or not treated at all. The unit of analysis is the practice itself — a jointly negotiated enterprise sustained over time by a group of people who develop shared ways of doing things, a shared repertoire, and a mutual engagement that holds the practice together.

From this starting point, Wenger builds an account of identity as a trajectory through practice. Who a person is, in this framework, is constituted by the histories of participation (and non-participation) that locate them in relation to one or more communities. Identity is neither a fixed interior property nor a role assigned from outside; it is the ongoing reconciliation of the multiple practices a person moves through and the forms of competence each one demands. The book spends considerable attention on how identities get formed at the boundaries between practices, how brokers carry elements across, and how non-participation — being kept out of, or choosing not to enter, a practice — is itself constitutive of who a person becomes.

The distinctive contribution of the solo 1998 volume is the framework of three modes of belonging: *engagement*, *imagination*, and *alignment*. Engagement is the familiar register — doing things together, producing the shared practice directly through mutual activity. Imagination is the mode by which a person locates themselves in relation to a community they do not physically inhabit and may never fully enter: picturing the larger enterprise, identifying with practitioners one does not know, orienting to a tradition or a field through images of what it is and who belongs to it. Alignment is the mode by which a person coordinates their activity with the trajectory of a community — following its standards, synchronizing with its rhythms, contributing to a direction — without necessarily doing the central work or engaging face-to-face with its members. The three modes are not a hierarchy and not substitutes for one another; they are distinct ways of belonging, each with its own work to do, and most real affiliations combine them in varying proportions.

This move is consequential. It gives theoretical standing to forms of belonging that earlier accounts of situated learning could accommodate only as weak or preparatory versions of full participation. A person who reads a field's literature, identifies with its practitioners, and orients their own work to its standards is belonging through imagination and alignment; the framework does not require them to be producing the core practice in order to count as participating in it. Boundary practices, constellations of interconnected practices, and the larger social landscapes through which communities are linked become analytically tractable rather than residual.

Wenger also develops a careful treatment of design and its limits. Practice cannot be designed directly; what can be designed are the conditions — infrastructure, artifacts, boundaries, rhythms — within which a practice may or may not take hold. The book closes by working this out for learning, for organizations, and for education, arguing that institutional arrangements succeed to the extent that they support the formation and sustenance of practices rather than substitute procedure for them. The result is a framework broad enough to account for how people come to belong to enterprises they encounter through many different registers, and for how meaning and identity are produced across the full range of those registers rather than only through direct, co-present work.
