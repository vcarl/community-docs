# The Effectiveness of Business Codes: A Critical Examination of Existing Studies and the Development of an Integrated Research Model

**Authors:** Muel Kaptein, Mark S. Schwartz
**Published:** 2008, Journal of Business Ethics, Vol. 77, pp. 111-127
**DOI:** 10.1007/s10551-006-9305-0
**Link:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-006-9305-0

## Abstract

Business codes are a widely used management instrument. Research into the effectiveness of business codes has, however, produced conflicting results. The main reasons for the divergent findings are: varying definitions of key terms; deficiencies in the empirical data and methodologies used; and a lack of theory. In this paper, we propose an integrated research model and suggest directions for future research.

## AI Summary

This is the most comprehensive meta-analysis of code of conduct (CoC) efficacy in the literature. Kaptein and Schwartz examined 79 empirical studies that investigated whether business codes of conduct are effective instruments for influencing employee behavior and organizational ethical performance.

The paper's primary contribution is methodological — it systematically catalogs the reasons why prior research produced such wildly divergent conclusions about code effectiveness. Of the 79 studies examined, 35% found codes effective, 16% found the relationship weak, 33% found no significant relationship, and 14% presented mixed results. The authors identify three categories of problems: definitional inconsistencies (what counts as a "code"? what does "effectiveness" mean?), empirical/methodological weaknesses (small samples, lack of controls, cross-sectional vs. longitudinal designs), and theoretical gaps (no coherent model linking code characteristics to behavioral outcomes).

They propose an integrated research model with fourteen factors organized into a causal chain: code creation → code content → code implementation → behavioral outcomes. The model distinguishes between actual vs. perceived code effectiveness, and argues researchers must control for organizational context variables (company size, industry, culture) that moderate the code-behavior relationship.

Key methodological insight: most prior studies used a crude binary comparison (companies with codes vs. without) rather than examining code quality, content specificity, enforcement mechanisms, or implementation depth. This binary approach masks enormous variation in what "having a code" actually means in practice.

The paper's conclusions are important for the broader CoC literature: the question is not "do codes work?" but rather "under what conditions, with what content, implemented how, do codes influence which behaviors?" The authors call for longitudinal studies, multi-level analysis, and more precise operationalization of both the independent variable (code characteristics) and the dependent variable (ethical behavior/performance). This framework has become foundational for subsequent empirical work on code effectiveness in both corporate and community governance contexts.
