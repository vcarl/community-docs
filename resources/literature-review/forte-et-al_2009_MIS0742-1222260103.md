# Decentralization in Wikipedia Governance

Forte, A., Larco, V., & Bruckman, A. (2009). Decentralization in Wikipedia Governance. *Journal of Management Information Systems*, 26(1), 49–72. https://doi.org/10.2753/MIS0742-1222260103

## Abstract

How does "self-governance" happen in Wikipedia? Through in-depth interviews with 20 individuals who have held a variety of responsibilities in the English-language Wikipedia, we obtained rich descriptions of how various forces produce and regulate social structures on the site. Although Wikipedia is sometimes portrayed as lacking oversight, our analysis describes Wikipedia as an organization with highly refined policies, norms, and a technological architecture that supports organizational ideals of consensus building and discussion. We describe how governance on the site is becoming increasingly decentralized as the community grows and how this is predicted by theories of commons-based governance developed in offline contexts. We also briefly examine local governance structures called WikiProjects through the example of WikiProject Military History, one of the oldest and most prolific projects on the site.

## AI Summary

Forte, Larco, and Bruckman (2009) investigate how the English-language Wikipedia came to govern itself as the project grew from a small coterie of contributors into one of the largest online collaborative communities in existence. Their method is qualitative: twenty in-depth interviews with participants who had held a range of roles, from ordinary editors to administrators, arbitrators, and WikiProject coordinators. The interview corpus allows the authors to reconstruct, from the participants' own accounts, how governance practices, policies, and local institutions emerged, mutated, and stabilized as the community's scale changed.

The central empirical finding is that Wikipedia's governance has decentralized over time, and that this decentralization was neither accidental nor incidental — it was a functional adaptation to growth. In the early project, a small group of highly involved editors (including Jimmy Wales) could adjudicate disputes, set norms, and steward content directly. As the number of editors and articles exploded, this centralized adjudication model became untenable. The authors describe three interlocking substitutes that took its place. First, the community accreted an increasingly refined body of *policy and guideline documents* — written artifacts that explained what was expected, how decisions were made, and how disputes were to be resolved. These documents did not replace discussion; they structured it, giving participants shared reference points that were independent of any particular person's authority. Second, the site's technological architecture — talk pages, revision histories, watchlists, noticeboards — made decision processes visible and auditable, so that participants who were not party to a given dispute could nonetheless understand what had happened and why. Third, and most strikingly, the community evolved *local* governance structures known as WikiProjects. The authors use WikiProject Military History as a detailed case: a topic-scoped sub-community that develops its own norms, its own review processes, and its own coordination rituals, all nested inside the broader policy framework. WikiProjects decentralize the cognitive and adjudicative load of governance by making most decisions legible and resolvable at a scale where participants can still meaningfully engage with one another.

The analytical frame the authors use is Elinor Ostrom's work on commons-based governance. Ostrom's study of long-lived collective institutions — irrigation districts, fisheries, forests — identified a set of design principles that recur in successful commons: clearly defined boundaries, rules adapted to local conditions, collective-choice arrangements for those affected, monitoring, graduated sanctions, low-cost conflict resolution, and nested layers of governance. Forte and colleagues map Wikipedia's observed structures onto these principles and find a remarkable correspondence. The policy corpus provides rules and boundaries; the revision system and noticeboards provide monitoring; the dispute-resolution ladder provides graduated sanctions and accessible conflict resolution; WikiProjects provide the nested layering that allows local self-government inside a larger polity. The paper is thus not merely descriptive but theoretically grounded: it argues that an online community, facing the governance stresses of scale, arrived by iterative adaptation at something close to the institutional form Ostrom's offline cases converged on.

This bears directly on the claim that scale changes the mechanics of trust. The axiom posits that as a community outgrows interpersonal knowledge, trust must be produced by legible process — documented rules, visible enforcement, accessible appeal — rather than by members knowing each other. Forte, Larco, and Bruckman provide an empirical demonstration of that shift inside a single community over time. Wikipedia's early participants could in fact rely on something closer to interpersonal knowledge among the core editors; its later participants could not, and the community's response was to build exactly the substrate the axiom describes. The paper also functions as a bridge in the literature: the axiom already cites Ostrom for the abstract design principles of durable commons governance, but Ostrom's cases are offline. This study is the empirical hand-off that shows those same principles operating in a massively distributed, networked, asynchronous online community. It shows that the move from interpersonal to institutional trust is not merely theorized for online communities but has been documented happening in one of the largest and most studied examples.

A secondary implication worth noting is the paper's treatment of decentralization as a *solution* to scale rather than a symptom of disorder. Observers who encountered late-stage Wikipedia sometimes characterized its sprawl of policies and subcommittees as bureaucratic overgrowth. The authors reframe this sprawl: the nested, documented, locally governed structure is what allows the project to continue functioning at a size where top-down or interpersonal coordination would collapse. For practitioners designing governance for communities expecting growth, the lesson is that process legibility and nested local institutions are not costs grudgingly accepted; they are the load-bearing substrate of trust at scale.
