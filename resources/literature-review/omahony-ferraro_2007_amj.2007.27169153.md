# The Emergence of Governance in an Open Source Community

O'Mahony, S., & Ferraro, F. (2007). The Emergence of Governance in an Open Source Community. *Academy of Management Journal*, 50(5), 1079–1106. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2007.27169153

## Abstract

Little is known about how communities producing collective goods govern themselves. In a multimethod study of one open source software community, the researchers found that members developed a shared basis of formal authority but limited it with democratic mechanisms that enabled experimentation with shifting conceptions of authority over time. When members settled on a shared conception of authority, it was more expansive than their original design. By blending bureaucratic and democratic mechanisms, the governance system evolved with the community's changing conceptions of authority.

## AI Summary

O'Mahony and Ferraro (2007) present a longitudinal, multimethod study of the Debian open source software community, tracing how its governance arrangements emerged and shifted over roughly a decade of growth. Debian is an unusually tractable case for this kind of work: the community produces a large collective good (a free operating system distribution), its membership is geographically dispersed and entirely volunteer, and virtually all of its deliberation — mailing list archives, constitutional drafts, election records, policy documents — is preserved in the public record. The authors exploit this archival depth alongside interviews and participant observation to reconstruct not just the final governance structure but the sequence of decisions and disputes that produced it.

The paper's central empirical contribution is to show that Debian did not converge on a single governance logic. Instead, as the community grew from a small founder-led project into a distributed organization of hundreds of maintainers, its members deliberately constructed a hybrid that blended bureaucratic and democratic mechanisms. The bureaucratic elements included a written constitution, codified roles with specified powers and limits, documented procedures for admitting new members, and explicit delineations of where authority resided. The democratic elements included elected leadership, fixed leadership terms, general resolutions by which the developer body could override officers, and procedures for constitutional amendment. Neither logic was adopted wholesale; each constrained the other. Elected officers held clearly defined and documented powers, but those powers were capped and revocable; bureaucratic roles existed, but their occupants were chosen and could be recalled by the membership.

The mechanism the authors identify behind this retooling is the breakdown of informal, interpersonal trust at scale. In Debian's early period, when the project was small enough for contributors to know one another directly, authority was produced through reputation, personal familiarity, and a shared sense of who had earned deference on which questions. Disputes could be resolved by appeal to mutually known facts about participants' histories and competencies. As the community grew and new contributors arrived who had no direct relationship with established members, this substrate thinned. Conflicts that would once have been resolved by interpersonal credit instead escalated because there was no shared basis for determining whose judgment should prevail. The response was not to try to scale the old mechanism but to replace it: authority was made legible by writing it down, bounded by making it revocable, and legitimated by making it elective. Process was built to do the work that acquaintance had previously done.

O'Mahony and Ferraro emphasize that this evolution was iterative and contested. Members experimented with different allocations of authority, observed how those allocations performed under stress, and revised the rules accordingly. The final conception of authority they settled on was more expansive than the minimalist arrangement the founders had initially envisioned — a finding that cuts against the common assumption that volunteer technical communities drift toward less structure over time. Growth, coupled with the need to sustain cooperation among strangers, pushed the community toward more structure, not less, and toward structure of a specifically legible and procedural kind.
