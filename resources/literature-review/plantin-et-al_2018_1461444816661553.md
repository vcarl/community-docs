# Infrastructure Studies Meet Platform Studies in the Age of Google and Facebook

Plantin, J.-C., Lagoze, C., Edwards, P. N., & Sandvig, C. (2018). Infrastructure Studies Meet Platform Studies in the Age of Google and Facebook. *New Media & Society*, 20(1), 293–310. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444816661553

## Abstract

Two theoretical approaches have recently emerged to characterize new digital objects of study in the media landscape: infrastructure studies and platform studies. Despite their separate origins and different features, we demonstrate in this article how the cross-articulation of these two perspectives improves our understanding of current digital media. We use case studies of the Open Web, Facebook, and Google to demonstrate that infrastructure studies provides a valuable approach to the evolution of shared, widely accessible systems and services of the type often provided or regulated by governments in the public interest. On the other hand, platform studies captures how communication and expression are both enabled and constrained by new digital systems and new media. In these environments, platform-based services acquire characteristics of infrastructure, while both new and existing infrastructures are built or reorganized on the logic of platforms. We conclude by underlining the potential of this combined framework for future case studies.

## AI Summary

Plantin, Lagoze, Edwards, and Sandvig (2018) bring together two bodies of scholarship that had until then largely talked past one another: infrastructure studies, with roots in science and technology studies and information science, and platform studies, centered in media studies. Infrastructure studies, through figures such as Susan Leigh Star and Paul Edwards himself, had developed a vocabulary for describing the large, shared sociotechnical systems — power grids, transportation networks, scientific cyberinfrastructures — on which modern life silently depends. Platform studies, emerging from work on video game consoles and software environments, had developed a vocabulary for describing how computational substrates shape the applications built on top of them: their affordances, constraints, programmability, and the business models organized around them. The authors argue that contemporary digital services such as Google and Facebook can no longer be adequately described by either vocabulary alone, because they have come to occupy both registers simultaneously.

The infrastructure-studies half of the synthesis is carefully foregrounded. Drawing on the tradition's core claims, the authors remind readers that infrastructure has several distinctive properties. It is ubiquitous and widely shared; it is reliable in a way that makes it taken for granted; it is *invisible until it breaks*, surfacing into conscious attention only through breakdown; it embeds standards — protocols, data formats, classification schemes — that silently shape what can be done on top of it; and it is typically understood as a public or quasi-public good, something whose provision is treated as a matter of common interest. Infrastructure, on this account, recedes from view precisely because it works, and its embedded standards quietly structure downstream action without being re-examined each time.

The platform-studies half emphasizes a different set of features. Platforms are programmable; they expose APIs; they court complementors; they are built on business models that extract value from the activity they host; and they exhibit specific affordances and constraints that shape user practice. Platform analysis, in other words, has been attentive to how these systems are *designed*, *governed*, and *monetized* by their owners — a register in which ownership, intention, and commercial interest are kept visible.

The authors' central move is to observe that Google and Facebook exhibit the ubiquity, reliance, invisibility, and standard-setting role that infrastructure studies identified, while retaining the proprietary control, commercial logic, and programmable affordance structure that platform studies identified. Through case studies of the Open Web, Facebook, and Google, they trace how platform-based services acquire infrastructural characteristics — becoming the default substrate for public communication, commerce, search, identity — while previously public or open infrastructures are rebuilt on platform logics, with APIs, terms of service, and extractive business models replacing open standards and common carriage. The result is a hybrid object: a system that functions like infrastructure in the lives of its users and dependents — taken for granted, relied upon, noticed only in breakdown — but that is owned, operated, and steered by a private firm with its own commercial objectives.

This hybridization has analytic consequences the authors draw out carefully. When a privately controlled service reaches infrastructural scale, its standards, defaults, and ranking decisions shape downstream action with the quiet ubiquity that infrastructure studies describes; users cease to perceive these decisions as decisions at all. Yet the entity making them is not a public utility, a standards body, or a government: it is a firm pursuing its own interests, whose choices about what to surface, whom to connect, what to prohibit, and what to measure are partisan in the precise sense that they serve particular ends. The invisibility-until-breakdown that benign public infrastructure earns by being reliable is, in the platform case, the same invisibility that insulates proprietary choices from scrutiny. A system can feel like an unremarked environmental condition while being, in fact, a contested and privately governed arrangement. Plantin and colleagues argue that studying these objects requires holding both registers at once — the infrastructural sensibility that attends to embedded standards and taken-for-grantedness, and the platform sensibility that keeps ownership, affordance design, and commercial interest in view.
