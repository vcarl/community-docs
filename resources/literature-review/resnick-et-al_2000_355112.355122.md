# Reputation Systems

Resnick, P., Kuwabara, K., Zeckhauser, R., & Friedman, E. (2000). Reputation Systems. *Communications of the ACM*, 43(12), 45–48. https://doi.org/10.1145/355112.355122

The article introduces and motivates the concept of a "reputation system" as a class of computer-mediated mechanism that gathers, distributes, and aggregates feedback about participants' past behavior in order to help strangers on the Internet decide whom to trust. The authors argue that such systems can, in principle, support cooperation among parties who have no prior relationship and no expectation of meeting again, thereby extending the range of trustworthy interaction far beyond the face-to-face settings in which reputation has traditionally operated. They sketch the requirements such a system must meet to be effective, draw illustrative examples from early online marketplaces (most prominently eBay's feedback profile), and catalog the practical problems — including feedback bias, retaliation, identity change, and the accuracy of aggregated scores — that reputation-system designers must confront.

## AI Summary

Resnick, Kuwabara, Zeckhauser, and Friedman (2000) offer one of the earliest compact articulations, in a computing venue, of why interpersonal trust mechanisms break down on the open Internet and what must be engineered to replace them. The piece is short and programmatic rather than empirical, but its framing has shaped more than two decades of subsequent work on online trust, marketplaces, and community design.

The central argument is that reputation — the shadow cast by past behavior over future interactions — has historically functioned as a substitute for direct verification in settings where people could reasonably expect to encounter one another again, or where word of past conduct traveled through overlapping social ties. In small, stable groups, this mechanism operates almost invisibly: community members accumulate knowledge about each other through repeated contact, and the prospect of future encounters disciplines present behavior. The authors observe that the Internet dissolves both conditions. Interactions are often between strangers, often one-shot, and often pseudonymous; the social ties that would otherwise carry word of misconduct are thin or absent. Under these conditions, the ambient reputational pressure that keeps small-group cooperation viable simply does not arise on its own.

A reputation system, in the authors' framing, is an engineered substitute for that missing ambient pressure. It collects feedback about participants' behavior in current interactions, makes that feedback available to others facing decisions about whom to deal with, and — if it works — causes participants to anticipate the reputational consequences of their choices. The authors identify three requirements for such a system to function. First, entities must be long-lived and must expect future interactions: without a continuous identity to accumulate reputation, and without a future in which that reputation matters, there is nothing for the system to discipline. Second, feedback about current interactions must actually be captured and distributed — which is a non-trivial design problem, since participants must be motivated to report, the reports must be aggregated in a way that reaches future counterparts, and the channel must be resistant to manipulation. Third, that distributed feedback must in fact guide the decisions of those who receive it; a system that produces ratings nobody consults provides no incentive to behave well.

Each requirement corresponds to a class of design failure the authors anticipate. Cheap, disposable identities defeat the first requirement by letting bad actors shed reputational history at will. Feedback that is sparse, biased, retaliatory, or inaccessible defeats the second. Ratings that are not salient at the moment of decision, or that users learn to distrust, defeat the third. The paper treats these not as peripheral problems but as the core engineering agenda: a reputation system succeeds or fails on whether its designers can satisfy all three conditions against adversarial pressure.

The underlying claim about scale is one of the paper's most durable contributions. The authors are explicit that individual verification does not scale. A participant on a large online platform cannot interview past counterparts of a prospective trading partner, cannot recognize repeat bad actors by sight, and cannot rely on mutual acquaintances to vouch. Something else has to do that work, and in the authors' account it must be a built system — a piece of infrastructure that collects, summarizes, and surfaces the information that word of mouth used to carry. This reframes trust, at scale, as a design problem rather than a social accident.

For the axiom that scale changes the mechanics of trust, this paper is one of the canonical early statements. Written in 2000, it predates most of the HCI community-design literature that later elaborated the point — including Kraut and Resnick's (2012) synthesis, in which Resnick himself returns to the theme at book length. The Resnick et al. article is narrower in scope (it is aimed primarily at online commerce and exchange, not at community governance), but the generative move is the same: once the group is too large for interpersonal knowledge to operate, trust must be produced by a legible, engineered process that collects signals, distributes them, and binds them to decisions. The specific process the paper proposes — feedback aggregation and display — is only one instance of that broader substitution, but the structural argument is already fully in view. Later work on moderation transparency, procedural legitimacy, and rule-based governance can be read as extending the same underlying claim into domains the authors do not themselves address.
