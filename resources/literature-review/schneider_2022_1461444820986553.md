# Admins, Mods, and Benevolent Dictators for Life: The Implicit Feudalism of Online Communities

Schneider, N. (2022). Admins, mods, and benevolent dictators for life: The implicit feudalism of online communities. *New Media & Society*, 24(9), 1965–1985. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444820986553

## Abstract

Online platforms train users to interact with each other through certain widespread interface designs. The article argues that an "implicit feudalism" informs the available options for community management on the dominant platforms for online communities. It is a pattern that grants user-administrators absolutist reign over their fiefdoms, with competition among them as the primary mechanism for quality control, typically under rules set by platform companies. In light of alternative management mechanisms with more democratic features, it becomes clear that implicit feudalism is not a necessary condition.

## AI Summary

Schneider (2022) names a pattern so ubiquitous in online community software that it tends to disappear from view: the small set of administrative roles and powers that nearly every major platform ships by default. A community — a subreddit, a Discord server, a Facebook group, an IRC channel, a Slack workspace, a forum — is created by a single user who then holds what amounts to sovereign authority over it. That user may appoint subordinate moderators, who hold delegated powers at the sovereign's pleasure and can be removed without recourse. Ordinary members have no standing in the governance structure at all: no vote, no term, no mechanism to recall or check those above them. Schneider argues that this arrangement is close enough to the structural logic of European feudalism — hereditary or appointed authority, held at the pleasure of a higher lord, with subjects bound to the land rather than party to its rule — that the analogy is diagnostic rather than merely rhetorical. He calls the pattern "implicit feudalism" because it is not presented as a political theory or defended as a governance choice; it is simply built into the tooling and adopted by anyone who uses the tooling.

The argument develops through a survey of the administrative models of the major platforms where online communities form. Across very different products — produced by different companies, aimed at different uses, shaped by different technical lineages — the same basic schema recurs. There is an owner or founder. There are moderators or admins appointed from above. There are users who interact with each other but have no formal role in how the community is governed. The convergence is striking enough that Schneider treats it as a fact about the medium rather than a fact about any one community. Product teams have not, for the most part, deliberated over governance forms and chosen monarchy; they have inherited a pattern, reproduced it, and shipped it as the path of least resistance.

A key move in the piece is the insistence that this is not neutral. The administrative model that platforms provide is the one communities actually get, because building anything else on top of a platform designed for owner-moderator hierarchies is prohibitively expensive. Features that a more democratic community would need — membership rolls that can vote, rotation of roles on a schedule, recall procedures, binding assemblies, proposal and amendment workflows — are not primitives in the available tooling. A community that wants them has to construct them laboriously out of bots, external spreadsheets, and side channels, and must accept that the platform itself will continue to treat the founder as the final authority regardless of any internal procedure the community enacts. The default shape of the software thus forecloses alternatives that would be well within the technical reach of the companies providing it.

Schneider sketches what a different design space would look like. He points to federated protocols, cooperative platforms, and governance experiments that have included rotation, sortition, appeal, and recall mechanisms. Some of these exist as research prototypes; some as niche projects; some as historical precedents from earlier eras of networked communication. The common feature is that they treat a community's governance form as a variable to be chosen, not a constant supplied by the host. The contrast underscores the central claim: the owner-admin-user hierarchy that feels like just how online communities work is a design choice, embedded in platform tooling, with political consequences. That choice can be made differently, and until it is, the range of governance forms available to most online communities is narrower than the communities themselves might prefer.
