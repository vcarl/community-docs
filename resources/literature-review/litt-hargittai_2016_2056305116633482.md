# The Imagined Audience on Social Network Sites

Litt, E., & Hargittai, E. (2016). The Imagined Audience on Social Network Sites. *Social Media + Society*, 2(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/2056305116633482

## Abstract

When people construct and share posts on social network sites like Facebook and Twitter, whom do they imagine as their audience? How do users describe this imagined audience? Do they have a sub-audience in mind (e.g., "friends who like reality television")? Do they share more broadly and abstractly (e.g., "the public")? Do such imaginings fluctuate each time a person posts? Using a mixed-methods approach involving a 2-month-long diary study of 119 diverse American adults and their 1,200 social network site posts, supplemented with follow-up interviews (N = 30), this study explores the imagined audience on social network sites. The findings reveal that even though users often interacted with large diverse audiences as they posted, they coped by envisioning either very broad abstract imagined audiences or more targeted specific imagined audiences composed of personal ties, professional ties, communal ties, and/or phantasmal ties. When people had target imagined audiences in mind, they were most often homogeneous and composed of people's friends and family. Users' imaginings typically fluctuated among these audience types as they posted even though the potential audience as per their posts' privacy settings often did not change. The findings provide a list of audience types, as well as detailed descriptions, examples, and frequencies on which future research can build. With people's online presence playing an important role for their reputations, these findings provide more insight into for whom people are managing their privacy and whom they have in mind as they share.

## AI Summary

Litt and Hargittai (2016) take an idea that had circulated primarily as theory — the "imagined audience," the mental construct of who one is addressing when posting to a potentially unbounded online public — and subject it to systematic empirical measurement. The concept had been developed most influentially in qualitative work on Twitter and context collapse, where researchers argued that social media users cannot literally know who is reading and must therefore imagine a target for each post. Litt and Hargittai set out to determine, across a population of ordinary adult users and across a corpus of their actual posts, who that imagined target tends to be in practice. Their central finding is that the imagined audience is almost never the full potential reach of a post; it is a much smaller, more specific mental picture, and in many cases it is a picture of people the user knows personally.

The methodological core of the study is a two-month diary study of 119 American adults, who logged 1,200 social network site posts in situ and, for each post, described whom they had in mind as they shared. This post-level data is supplemented by 30 follow-up interviews. Because respondents reported on actual posts rather than hypothetical ones, and because they did so close in time to posting rather than retrospectively, the data capture the imagined audience as it operates at the moment of composition rather than as a summary self-description. The sample is recruited to be demographically diverse rather than drawn from student populations that had dominated prior work, which makes the distributions the authors report more broadly informative.

From this data the authors derive a typology of imagined-audience types. Some posts are addressed to a broad, abstract audience — "everyone," "the public," "whoever sees it" — with no specific persons in view. Other posts are addressed to more targeted, specific audiences, which Litt and Hargittai sort into personal ties (friends and family), professional ties (colleagues, professional contacts), communal ties (people who share an interest, activity, or identity), and phantasmal ties (people imagined by the poster but not actually connected to them on the platform, such as an absent relative or a celebrity). When users named a specific imagined audience, it was overwhelmingly a small and homogeneous one, and most often a subset of personal ties. The plural, overlapping network the platform technically delivers a post to is not the network the user is picturing.

A second finding sharpens the first. Across the same user's posts, the imagined audience fluctuates — different posts are directed at different subsets — even as the privacy settings that determine actual reach typically remain unchanged. The user's sense of who is being addressed moves from post to post; the platform's delivery does not. The gap between imagined and actual audience is therefore not a single static misalignment but a dynamic one, renewed with each post, because users continue to compose for a specific internal picture while the system continues to distribute to the full permitted set.

The paper treats this gap as a substantive feature of how people manage self-presentation and privacy online, not as a user error. Envisioning a smaller, specific audience is how people cope with the cognitive and social impossibility of addressing the full technical reach of a post; it is the mechanism that makes posting feel tractable at all. But the corollary is that the audience users believe they are managing their self-presentation for is systematically a small fraction of the audience that actually receives their content. The authors' typology and frequencies give subsequent work a concrete empirical baseline — a set of named audience types and their relative prevalences — against which later studies of self-presentation, privacy behavior, and platform design can be calibrated.
