# Polis: Scaling Deliberation by Mapping High Dimensional Opinion Spaces

Small, C., Bjorkegren, M., Erkkilä, T., Shaw, L., & Megill, C. (2021). Polis: Scaling Deliberation by Mapping High Dimensional Opinion Spaces. *Recerca: Revista de Pensament i Anàlisi*, 26(2). https://doi.org/10.6035/recerca.5516

## Abstract

The following abstract was reconstructed from the publisher's article page; the original verbatim abstract was not cleanly retrievable, so this is a paraphrase of the publisher-provided summary rather than a direct quotation. Polis is described as an open-source web application for collecting and synthesizing feedback at scale, developed to address the long-standing problem that deliberative and participatory approaches to democracy have historically struggled to operate beyond the scale of a city-state or small group. The authors argue that the application — which combines participant-generated statements, agree/disagree/pass voting, machine learning, and data visualization — has demonstrated the capacity to build shared understanding among participants, discourage unproductive participation such as trolling, and identify points of consensus across large and opinion-diverse populations. The paper documents Polis deployments across journalistic research, academic studies, and formal governmental decision-making bodies, including cases where outputs of a Polis conversation have directly informed legislation at local and national levels.

## AI Summary

Small, Bjorkegren, Erkkilä, Shaw, and Megill (2021) present Polis as both a piece of civic technology and a theoretical response to a problem that has haunted democratic theory since antiquity: how to preserve the qualities of deliberation — mutual understanding, surfacing of minority views, identification of genuine consensus — when the participating population is too large for face-to-face interaction. The paper is simultaneously a system description, a report on deployments, and an argument about what it means to "scale" deliberation at all.

As a system, Polis operates on a simple participant-facing surface: users submit short written statements (seed comments plus anything participants add during the conversation), and all participants vote agree, disagree, or pass on each statement they see. The machinery behind that surface is more ambitious. Each participant becomes a high-dimensional vector of votes; the full participant set becomes a point cloud in opinion space. Polis applies dimensionality reduction and clustering to project that cloud into two dimensions and to identify opinion groups — clusters of participants whose voting patterns resemble one another. The resulting visualizations expose two distinct objects that are difficult to see at scale by any other means: the statements on which opinion clusters diverge (the axes of real disagreement), and the statements on which otherwise-divergent clusters agree (points of cross-group consensus). The system is designed so that the second category — consensus across factions — becomes legible and prioritizable, rather than buried under volume.

The theoretical argument the authors advance is that this substitution is itself what "scaling deliberation" must mean. Face-to-face deliberation in small groups produces its qualities through a specific cognitive and social process: people hear each other, notice where they agree, update in response to argument. That process does not mechanically extend to thousands or millions of participants; the bandwidth is simply not there. The authors' claim is that dimensionality reduction and visualization can stand in for some — not all — of those small-group processes. Participants cannot listen to everyone else, but the map can show them where they sit relative to everyone else, and the map can show conveners which statements cut across factional lines. The deliberative goods of legibility, mutual recognition, and consensus-finding are reconstructed through a different substrate rather than attempted at impossible fidelity.

The deployment evidence discussed in the paper — most prominently the vTaiwan process, in which Polis conversations were used as structured inputs into Taiwanese regulatory decisions, alongside journalistic and governmental uses in other jurisdictions — is the paper's empirical anchor. Those cases matter less for any single quantitative result than for what they demonstrate about process legitimacy: large, opinion-diverse publics engaging with a structured artifact whose workings are visible, whose outputs are auditable, and whose consensus findings can be traced back to the underlying vote distribution. Participants and observers can inspect why a given statement was flagged as consensus. That inspectability is itself a process signal.

This bears directly on the axiom that scale changes the mechanics of trust. The axiom's force is that once a community exceeds the size at which members can hold working models of each other, trust has to be produced through legible process rather than interpersonal knowledge. Polis is a concrete, post-2020 technical substrate for exactly that kind of legible process, operating at population sizes where the interpersonal mechanism is hopelessly inapplicable. It is also a useful counter to a common misreading of the axiom: that process-based trust must mean bureaucratic hierarchy, committees, and formal appeals machinery. Polis shows that the process signals the axiom requires — documented inputs, visible decision logic, auditable outputs, explicit surfacing of minority and consensus positions — can be produced by tooling tailored to the actual shape of the participating population, rather than by borrowing institutional forms from small-group governance and attempting to grow them. The paper is therefore less a case for Polis specifically than evidence that the design space for legible-process-at-scale is larger and more tractable than it appeared when the axiom's underlying empirical literature (Ostrom, Kraut and Resnick) was assembled. New instruments can generate trust signals adapted to the population size rather than retrofitted from mechanisms evolved for smaller ones.
