# Applications of Social Identity Theory to Research and Design in Computer-Supported Cooperative Work

Seering, J., Ng, F., Yao, Z., & Kaufman, G. (2018). Applications of Social Identity Theory to Research and Design in Computer-Supported Cooperative Work. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 2(CSCW), Article 201. https://doi.org/10.1145/3274771

## Abstract

Research in computer-supported cooperative work has historically focused on behaviors of individuals at scale, using frames of interpersonal interaction such as Goffman's theories of self-presentation. These frames prioritize research detailing the characteristics, personal identities, and behaviors of large numbers of interacting individuals, while the social identity concepts that lead to intra- and inter-group dynamics have received far less attention. We argue that the emergent properties of self-categorization and social identity, which are particularly fluid and complex in online spaces, provide a complementary perspective with which to re-examine traditional topics in social computing. We discuss the applicability of the Social Identity Perspective to both established and new research domains in CSCW, proposing alternative perspectives on self-presentation, social support, collaboration, misbehavior, and leadership. We propose a set of methodological considerations derived from this body of theories and accompanying empirical work. We close by considering how broad concepts and lessons from social identity provide a valuable lens for inspiring future work in CSCW.

## AI Summary

Seering, Ng, Yao, and Kaufman (2018) offer a review and theoretical reorientation rather than a primary empirical study. Their argument is that the dominant frame in computer-supported cooperative work has been individualist — treating online platforms as aggregations of discrete users whose self-presentation, motivations, and behaviors are best analyzed at the level of the person — and that this frame has left a whole class of phenomena undertheorized. The phenomena they have in mind are those that arise specifically from membership in a group: the ways in which being categorized, or self-categorizing, as part of a "we" changes how a person perceives, behaves, and is perceived by others. To recover these phenomena the authors reach back to the social identity tradition associated with Tajfel and Turner and the self-categorization theory that developed out of it, and they work through what that tradition implies for CSCW.

The review opens with a careful restatement of the core principles of the social identity perspective. A person carries both a personal identity — the attributes that distinguish them as a unique individual — and a set of social identities corresponding to the groups they are, or understand themselves to be, part of. Which identity is active at a given moment is situationally determined; the same person may foreground a professional identity in one context, a fan identity in another, an ideological identity in a third. When a social identity becomes salient, the person's self-concept shifts: they begin to perceive themselves and others in terms of group categories rather than individual attributes, they attribute to themselves the prototypical characteristics of the in-group, and their behavior is shaped by the norms they take to define that group. This shift — depersonalization, in Turner's term — is the mechanism by which group-level phenomena become possible at all.

From this foundation the authors develop a crucial distinction that runs through the rest of the review: identification with a group is analytically separate from behavioral participation in it. A person can categorize themselves as a member, derive self-esteem and meaning from the membership, and internalize the group's norms without engaging in the visible activities typically treated as evidence of membership. In the social identity tradition, the cognitive act of categorization is itself constitutive; belonging is produced by the self-ascription, not only by the contribution. This has direct implications for how online communities are studied. Logs of posts, edits, and interactions capture behavioral engagement but not identification; the two can diverge sharply, and a research apparatus tuned only to the former will systematically miss the latter.

The paper then works through five domains — self-presentation, social support, collaboration, misbehavior, and leadership — and in each shows what comes into view once the social-identity lens is applied. Self-presentation ceases to be an individual performance and becomes the display of prototypical group features. Social support is reframed as something received from an in-group rather than from generic others, with correspondingly different effects. Collaboration is structured by the boundaries of the groups participants perceive themselves to belong to. Misbehavior is often inter-group behavior driven by identification with one side of a perceived divide, not individual deviance. Leadership is the occupancy of a prototypical position within the group as its members construe it, rather than the exercise of formal authority.

The authors also take pains to note that identification does not arise automatically from co-presence. Merely placing people on the same platform, or giving them shared tools, does not produce a shared social identity; the conditions under which identification forms, strengthens, and weakens are themselves a subject for research and design. The review closes with methodological recommendations — measuring identification directly, attending to the contextual cues that make categories salient, distinguishing cognitive belonging from observable activity — that follow from the theoretical argument and that would let CSCW research register the phenomena the prior individualist frame left invisible.
