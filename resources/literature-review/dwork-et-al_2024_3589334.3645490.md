# Content Moderation and the Formation of Online Communities: A Theoretical Framework

**Authors:** Cynthia Dwork, Chris Hays, Jon Kleinberg, Manish Raghavan
**Published:** 2024, WWW '24: Proceedings of the ACM Web Conference 2024, pp. 1307-1317
**DOI:** 10.1145/3589334.3645490
**Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3589334.3645490

## Abstract

We study the impact of content moderation policies in online communities. In our theoretical model, a platform chooses a content moderation policy and individuals choose whether or not to participate in the community according to the fraction of user content that aligns with their preferences. The effects of content moderation, at first blush, might seem obvious: platform speech is restricted. However, when user participation decisions are taken into account, its effects can be more subtle — and counter-intuitive. For example, our model can straightforwardly demonstrate how moderation policies may increase participation and/or diversify content available on the platform. In our analysis, we explore a rich set of interconnected phenomena related to content moderation in online communities. We first characterize the effectiveness of a natural class of moderation policies for creating and sustaining communities. Building on this, we explore how resource-limited or ideological platforms might set policies, how communities are affected by differing levels of personalization, and competition between platforms. Our model provides a vocabulary and mathematically tractable framework for analyzing platform decisions about content moderation.

## AI Summary

This paper from the ACM Web Conference 2024 provides a mathematical framework for understanding how content moderation policies interact with user participation decisions to shape online communities. The authors — a team of prominent computer scientists from Harvard, MIT, and Cornell — apply theoretical modeling to a question that has primarily been studied empirically.

The methodology is theoretical and mathematical. The researchers construct a formal model in which a platform selects a content moderation policy (determining which types of content are removed or suppressed), and individuals then decide whether to participate based on the resulting content mix. Each user has preferences over the types of content they want to see, and they join or leave the platform based on whether the actual content distribution aligns sufficiently with their preferences.

The model's central insight is that content moderation has both direct and indirect effects. The direct effect is obvious: moderation restricts speech by removing certain content. The indirect effect, however, is less intuitive: by changing the content mix, moderation changes which users find the platform attractive, which in turn changes who participates, which further changes the content mix. This feedback loop can produce counter-intuitive outcomes.

Specifically, the model demonstrates three key counter-intuitive results. First, moderation can increase total participation: by removing content that drives away a large group of potential users, a platform can gain more participants than it loses. Second, moderation can diversify content: removing extreme content can make the platform more hospitable to moderate voices, actually broadening the range of viewpoints expressed. Third, the lack of moderation can lead to community collapse: without moderation, platforms can experience death spirals where the most extreme voices drive away moderate participants, which in turn makes the platform even more extreme, driving away more moderates, and so on.

The researchers extend their model to examine several practical scenarios. For resource-limited platforms (which cannot moderate all content), they derive optimal strategies for allocating limited moderation resources. For ideological platforms (which have specific preferences about community composition), they analyze how platforms might use moderation strategically. They also examine personalization (algorithmic content filtering as a substitute for or complement to moderation) and inter-platform competition (where users can migrate between platforms with different moderation policies).

The study's conclusions reframe the content moderation debate: rather than viewing moderation as a simple trade-off between safety and free expression, the model shows that moderation is better understood as a community design tool that shapes who participates and what content is produced. The mathematical framework provides a vocabulary for analyzing platform decisions and predicting their effects on community formation and sustainability. This work bridges theoretical computer science and platform governance, offering formal tools for reasoning about policy choices that are typically discussed in purely qualitative terms.
