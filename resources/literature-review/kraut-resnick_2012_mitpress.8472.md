# Building Successful Online Communities

Kraut, R. E., & Resnick, P. (2012). *Building Successful Online Communities: Evidence-Based Social Design*. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/8472.001.0001

## Abstract

*Building Successful Online Communities* offers lessons from theory and empirical research in the social sciences that can improve the design of online communities. Kraut and Resnick, writing with Sara Kiesler, Moira Burke, Yan Chen, Niki Kittur, Joseph Konstan, Yuqing Ren, and John Riedl, draw on literature in social psychology, economics, and human–computer interaction, as well as their own research, translating general findings into specific, testable design claims. They explain, for example, how to encourage information contributions by applying theories of public goods, and how to build members' commitment by applying theories of interpersonal bond formation and group identification. For each claim, the authors provide supporting evidence from theory, experiments, or observational studies, and they illustrate the claims with examples drawn from a range of online communities. The book treats community design as an engineering discipline informed by cumulative social-science research rather than as a matter of intuition or folklore, and it has become a standard reference for practitioners and researchers working on the design of sociotechnical systems. ## AI Summary

Kraut and Resnick (2012) consolidate roughly a decade of HCI, CSCW, social-psychology, and behavioral-economics research into a single evidence-based reference for the design of online communities. The book's distinctive methodological move is to organize that literature around *design claims*: each chapter states a series of testable propositions of the form "if a community does X, then Y is more likely," grounds each claim in the empirical and theoretical work that supports it, and then discusses the design implications and trade-offs that follow. This format — claim, evidence, implication, caveat — is consistent across chapters and gives the book the character of a handbook in which every recommendation carries its provenance with it. Where the literature is thin or the findings are mixed, the authors say so; where a claim has been tested across multiple studies and settings, they mark it as well-supported. The effect is to transform a scattered empirical record into a set of design propositions a practitioner can actually apply, and to make the underlying evidence inspectable rather than asserted.

Much of the book's theoretical weight sits in its treatment of commitment, which the authors refuse to treat as a single construct. Drawing on work in group psychology, organizational behavior, and social identity theory, they distinguish several distinct bases on which a member can become attached to a community, and they argue that different bases call for different design moves. A central contrast runs between *identity-based* commitment — attachment to the group as a category, built through shared purpose, group identity markers, boundaries between insiders and outsiders, and cues that signal what the collective stands for — and *bond-based* commitment, attachment to specific other members, built through repeated interaction, personal disclosure, reciprocity, and the accumulation of interpersonal history. A third strand, drawn from organizational-commitment theory, separates *normative* commitment (a felt obligation to remain) from *affective* commitment (emotional attachment) and from continuance commitment (costs of leaving). The design implications diverge: identity-based attachment is cultivated through visible group symbols, shared tasks, intergroup contrast, and narratives about the community's distinctiveness, while bond-based attachment is cultivated by making individual members visible to one another, supporting dyadic interaction, surfacing shared history, and reducing the friction of repeat contact. A community that leans on one mechanism and neglects the other produces a recognizable failure mode, and the authors are explicit about which design levers feed which channel.

A related contribution is the book's sustained attention to members who do not actively post or contribute. Rather than treating non-contribution as a pathology to be corrected, the authors analyze the conditions under which observation, reading, and light engagement are themselves forms of participation, and they examine which design choices convert low-intensity presence into deeper attachment over time. They discuss how visibility of others' activity, the legibility of group norms, the availability of low-cost participation affordances, and the design of membership signals shape whether someone on the edge of a community drifts away, stabilizes as an audience member, or gradually moves toward contribution. The treatment is careful not to assume that every member should become a core contributor; instead, it asks what kinds of commitment are available to members at different levels of engagement and how design choices open or foreclose those paths.

Surrounding these chapters are treatments of starting new communities, encouraging contribution, regulating behavior, and dealing with newcomers, each handled with the same claim-and-evidence structure. The book's synthesis is not exhaustive — it reflects the empirical base available by the early 2010s — but its method has proven durable. By making each recommendation traceable to the study or theory behind it, Kraut and Resnick produced a reference that can be updated, contested, and extended claim by claim rather than read as a unified doctrine, and that treats commitment as the multidimensional construct the underlying research shows it to be.
