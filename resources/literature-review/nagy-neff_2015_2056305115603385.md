# Imagined Affordance: Reconstructing a Keyword for Communication Theory

Nagy, P., & Neff, G. (2015). Imagined Affordance: Reconstructing a Keyword for Communication Theory. *Social Media + Society*, 1(2). https://doi.org/10.1177/2056305115603385

## Abstract

In this essay, the authors reconstruct a keyword for communication—affordance. Affordance, adopted from ecological psychology, is now widely used in technology studies, yet the term lacks a clear definition. This is especially problematic for scholars grappling with how to theorize the relationship between technology and sociality for complex socio-technical systems such as machine-learning algorithms, pervasive computing, the Internet of Things, and other such "smart" innovations. To solve this, they develop the concept of imagined affordance. Imagined affordances emerge between users' perceptions, attitudes, and expectations; between the materiality and functionality of technologies; and between the intentions and perceptions of designers. The authors use imagined affordance to evoke the importance of imagination in affordances—expectations for technology that are not fully realized in conscious, rational knowledge, and to distinguish their process-oriented, socio-technical definition of affordance from the "imagined" consensus of the field around a flimsier use of the term, in order to better capture the importance of mediation, materiality, and affect. They suggest that imagined affordance helps to theorize the duality of materiality and communication technology: namely, that people shape their media environments, perceive them, and have agency within them because of imagined affordances.

## AI Summary

Nagy and Neff (2015) intervene in a term that had by the mid-2010s become ubiquitous in communication and technology studies while losing most of its analytic bite. "Affordance" entered the field from James Gibson's ecological psychology, where it named the action possibilities an environment offered a perceiving organism, and was then inflected through Donald Norman's design writing, where it came to describe features of artifacts that suggested their own use. As the term crossed into studies of digital media, the authors argue, it drifted toward a flattened reading in which affordances were treated as properties of an object — stable capabilities inhering in a platform, a device, or an interface, waiting to be read off by any competent observer. Under this flattened use, researchers could speak of "the affordances of Twitter" or "the affordances of the smartphone" as if these were fixed lists, and could draw causal conclusions about social behavior directly from them.

The authors' objection is that this usage quietly smuggles in a hard technological determinism. If an affordance is simply a property of the object, then the technology itself becomes the explanation for what users do, and the analyst's task reduces to cataloguing features. Nagy and Neff argue that this misrepresents both Gibson's original formulation, which was explicitly relational — an affordance existed between organism and environment — and the empirical reality of contemporary sociotechnical systems, where opaque algorithms, ambient computing, and data-driven personalization make it increasingly difficult to say what the "object" even is, much less to enumerate its features in advance.

Their reconstruction is tripartite. An imagined affordance, in their account, emerges at the intersection of three distinct contributions. First, the material and functional constraints of the technology itself — what the code, the interface, the sensors, the protocol actually permit and prohibit. Second, the intentions and perceptions of designers — the mental models, assumptions, and strategic goals baked into the artifact during its production, which shape but do not fully determine how it behaves. Third, and the move the authors most want to foreground, the perceptions, expectations, and imaginative work of users — the mental models they bring to the technology, the uses they project onto it, the affects and anticipations that color their encounter with it. What the technology does in practice is produced by all three contributions operating together. None of them alone is sufficient, and none of them is reducible to the others.

The concept's power, in the authors' hands, is that it makes space for user imagination without abandoning materiality. Users are not free to project any meaning onto any artifact; the material constraints are real and set outer limits. But within those limits, what an affordance is depends on how it is perceived and taken up. A feature that designers intended one way may be reimagined and repurposed; a feature that exists in the code may be invisible to users who do not imagine it as available; a feature that does not exist may nonetheless be acted upon because users believe it does. This is why, the authors argue, the term must carry "imagined" as a modifier: to mark that the affordance is never simply read off the object, and that the work of perception, expectation, and affect is constitutive rather than incidental.

The essay's broader implication is methodological. Analysis of sociotechnical systems cannot proceed by auditing features alone, nor by treating users as blank recipients of whatever a technology dictates. It must hold designer intent, material constraint, and user imagination in view at once, and track how each contributes to what the technology comes to mean and do in practice. Communication technologies acquire their social character through this ongoing mutual shaping, not through the unilateral action of any single layer.
