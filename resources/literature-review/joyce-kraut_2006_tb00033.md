# Predicting Continued Participation in Newsgroups

Joyce, E., & Kraut, R. E. (2006). Predicting Continued Participation in Newsgroups. *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*, 11(3), 723–747. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2006.00033.x

## Abstract

Turnover in online communities is very high, with most people who initially post a message to an online community never contributing again. In this paper, we test whether the responses that newcomers receive to their first posts influence the extent to which they continue to participate. The data come from initial posts made by 2,777 newcomers to six public newsgroups. We coded the content and valence of the initial post and its first response, if it received one, to see if these factors influenced newcomers' likelihood of posting again. Approximately 61% of newcomers received a reply to their initial post, and those who got a reply were 12% more likely to post to the community again; their probability of posting again increased from 44% to 56%. They were more likely to receive a response if they asked a question or wrote a longer post. Surprisingly, the quality of the response they received—its emotional tone and whether it answered a newcomer's question—did not influence the likelihood of the newcomer's posting again.

## AI Summary

Joyce and Kraut (2006) take up a durable empirical puzzle about online communities: the vast majority of people who post once never post again. The question they set for themselves is whether the community's own behavior — specifically, how it responds to a newcomer's first message — affects whether that newcomer comes back. Their answer, drawn from archival analysis of public Usenet newsgroups, is that it does, but in a narrower and stranger way than the intuitive account predicts. Simply getting any reply matters. The content of that reply, including whether it is warm or hostile and whether it answers the question asked, does not measurably change the probability of return within the window they study.

The methodology is central to how the paper should be read. Rather than surveying participants or running an experimental manipulation, the authors work from a full longitudinal trace of behavior. They identify 2,777 individuals making their first observable post to one of six newsgroups, capture the initial post and its first response (if any), and then follow each newcomer forward in time to observe whether they post again. Each initial post and each first response is hand-coded along several dimensions: what kind of message it was (question, opinion, self-introduction, request), its length, its emotional tone, and — for responses — whether the reply was substantive, hostile, welcoming, indifferent, or whether the question was actually answered. This produces a dataset in which features of the newcomer's entry and features of the community's reception can be entered as predictors of continued participation, with controls for characteristics of the newcomer's own post.

The headline result is that receiving a reply raises the probability of posting again from 44% to 56% — a twelve-point lift that holds after adjusting for what the newcomer wrote. Newcomers who asked a question or wrote at greater length were more likely to attract a reply in the first place, giving post form an indirect effect on retention through its effect on whether the community engaged. The non-finding is equally important. Once a reply occurred, the character of that reply did not predict the newcomer's return. Hostile responses, indifferent responses, warm responses, and responses that actually answered the question were statistically indistinguishable in their effect on whether the newcomer came back. What mattered, in the data, was whether anyone responded at all.

The authors interpret this through a social-exchange and reinforcement lens: the first post is a probe, and a reply — almost regardless of its contents — signals that the probe registered, that the community is inhabited, and that further posts are likely to be noticed. Silence, by contrast, communicates that contributions disappear into a void, and most newcomers treated that signal as decisive. The design holds constant, statistically, the newcomer's own behavior, so the differential return rates cannot be explained away as more capable newcomers both attracting replies and being more likely to persist for independent reasons; the community's response itself carries predictive weight.

Several implications follow from the shape of the finding. Whatever sorting occurs at the threshold of these communities is operating early — at the level of whether a first post is engaged at all — and operating on the population of people who have already mustered the effort to post once. Because reply likelihood depends partly on post form, the members who know how to write a question-shaped, appropriately-sized first post are disproportionately the ones who receive the signal that invites them to stay. The mechanism is not adjudication of newcomer quality by tone or welcome but a more austere filter: the community replies to some opening moves and not others, and those whose openings go unanswered mostly leave. The pattern is observable in routinely produced behavioral traces, without asking participants anything, and it is reproducible wherever similar archival records exist.
