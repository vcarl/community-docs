# Laboratories of Oligarchy? How the Iron Law Extends to Peer Production

Shaw, A., & Hill, B. M. (2014). Laboratories of Oligarchy? How the Iron Law Extends to Peer Production. *Journal of Communication*, 64(2), 215–238. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12082

## Abstract

Peer production projects such as Wikipedia have inspired voluntary associations, collectives, social movements, and scholars to embrace open online collaboration as a model of democratic organization. However, many such projects exhibit entrenched leadership and participation inequalities that raise questions about the robustness of their democratic aspirations. Shaw and Hill draw on Robert Michels' classical "iron law of oligarchy" to examine whether peer production communities, despite being organized around open participation, nonetheless concentrate authority among a small core as they grow. Using an exhaustive dataset of internal processes drawn from a sample of hundreds of wikis, the authors test whether oligarchic tendencies strengthen as contributor bases expand. They find support for Michels' iron law: peer production entails oligarchic organizational forms, complicating accounts that treat open collaborative production as an alternative to the hierarchical dynamics observed in traditional organizations.

## AI Summary

Shaw and Hill (2014) revisit Robert Michels' "iron law of oligarchy" — the claim, originating in early twentieth-century political sociology, that any sufficiently large organization, regardless of its democratic intentions, tends to concentrate power in a small, self-perpetuating leadership. Michels derived this from studies of socialist parties and trade unions, groups explicitly committed to participatory ideals. Shaw and Hill ask whether the same dynamic appears in online peer production, a domain that many theorists and practitioners have hoped would escape the organizational gravity Michels described. Their answer, based on systematic empirical analysis, is that peer production does not escape it. Open participation as a design principle is not sufficient to prevent oligarchic drift.

The empirical basis for the argument is notable in scope. Rather than relying on a single case — Wikipedia has dominated much of the prior literature — the authors draw on internal process data from a large sample of wikis running on the MediaWiki platform. This comparative design allows them to examine how participation and authority distribute across communities at different sizes and stages of development, treating each wiki as an organizational unit whose governance dynamics can be measured. By looking across many communities rather than within one, they can distinguish features specific to any single project from patterns that recur as peer-production communities grow.

The central finding is that growth in participation is accompanied by increasing concentration of authority. As wikis attract more contributors, the distribution of administrative power, influence over decisions, and control over rules does not flatten outward with the contributor base; instead, it narrows. Early and highly engaged members accumulate formal roles, informal standing, and the procedural familiarity that makes them disproportionately capable of shaping outcomes. Newcomers enter a structure already shaped, and the openness of the technical platform does not translate into openness of the governance layer. The authors interpret this as a direct extension of Michels into a domain that was supposed to falsify him. Peer production is not a refutation of the iron law; it is another instance of it.

For the axiom that scale changes the mechanics of trust, this work is a critical boundary condition. The axiom argues that at scale, interpersonal trust fails and must be replaced by legible process — documented rules, consistent enforcement, visible legitimacy — to produce trust for members who cannot individually know one another. Shaw and Hill do not contradict this diagnosis, but they name a failure mode the axiom must account for. Process, by itself, does not neutralize oligarchic drift. A community can have documented rules, appeal mechanisms, and transparent logs, and still concentrate decisive authority among early and self-selecting members who understand the process apparatus better than anyone else does. The apparatus that is meant to substitute for interpersonal trust can, in practice, become the terrain on which insider advantage is exercised. Legibility of process is a necessary condition for trust at scale; it is not a sufficient one.

The paper also sharpens what the axiom describes as the experience of established versus peripheral members. The axiom notes that established members may continue to feel their community's trust mechanisms are working even as newer members experience something breaking down. Shaw and Hill supply the empirical backing for that intuition. In a community subject to oligarchic concentration, established members occupy the positions from which authority operates; the process feels coherent to them because they are fluent in it and, often, because they shape its application. Peripheral members encounter the same process as an opaque machinery whose operators are not accountable in any mechanism they can reach. The subjective legitimacy gap between core and periphery is not a perception error; it tracks a real distribution of power.

The implication for community design is not to abandon process in favor of something looser — that path leads directly to the interpersonal-trust failures the axiom already describes — but to treat process design as itself a site where oligarchic dynamics can take hold. Documented rules must be accompanied by mechanisms that actively distribute authority, rotate roles, lower the cost of meaningful participation for newcomers, and surface decisions that would otherwise be made invisibly by the core. Shaw and Hill's contribution is to make clear that the work of building trust at scale is not finished when process becomes legible. Legibility is the substrate on which a second, harder question — who actually governs — must then be answered.
