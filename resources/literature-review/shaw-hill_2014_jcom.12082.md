# Laboratories of Oligarchy? How the Iron Law Extends to Peer Production

Shaw, A., & Hill, B. M. (2014). Laboratories of Oligarchy? How the Iron Law Extends to Peer Production. *Journal of Communication*, 64(2), 215–238. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12082

## Abstract

Peer production projects such as Wikipedia have inspired voluntary associations, collectives, social movements, and scholars to embrace open online collaboration as a model of democratic organization. However, many such projects exhibit entrenched leadership and participation inequalities that raise questions about the robustness of their democratic aspirations. Shaw and Hill draw on Robert Michels' classical "iron law of oligarchy" to examine whether peer production communities, despite being organized around open participation, nonetheless concentrate authority among a small core as they grow. Using an exhaustive dataset of internal processes drawn from a sample of hundreds of wikis, the authors test whether oligarchic tendencies strengthen as contributor bases expand. They find support for Michels' iron law: peer production entails oligarchic organizational forms, complicating accounts that treat open collaborative production as an alternative to the hierarchical dynamics observed in traditional organizations.

## AI Summary

Shaw and Hill (2014) revisit Robert Michels' "iron law of oligarchy" — the claim, originating in early twentieth-century political sociology, that any sufficiently large organization, regardless of its democratic intentions, tends to concentrate power in a small, self-perpetuating leadership. Michels derived this from studies of socialist parties and trade unions, groups explicitly committed to participatory ideals. Shaw and Hill ask whether the same dynamic appears in online peer production, a domain many theorists and practitioners have hoped would escape the organizational gravity Michels described. Their answer, based on systematic empirical analysis, is that peer production does not escape it. Open participation as a design principle is not sufficient to prevent oligarchic drift.

The empirical basis for the argument is notable in scope. Rather than relying on a single case — Wikipedia has dominated much of the prior literature — the authors draw on internal process data from a large sample of wikis running on the MediaWiki platform. This comparative design lets them examine how participation and authority distribute across communities at different sizes and stages of development, treating each wiki as an organizational unit whose governance dynamics can be measured. By looking across many communities rather than within one, they can distinguish features specific to any single project from patterns that recur as peer-production communities grow.

The central finding is that growth in participation is accompanied by increasing concentration of authority. As wikis attract more contributors, the distribution of administrative power, influence over decisions, and control over rules does not flatten outward with the contributor base; instead, it narrows. Early and highly engaged members accumulate formal roles, informal standing, and the procedural familiarity that makes them disproportionately capable of shaping outcomes. Newcomers enter a structure already shaped, and the openness of the technical platform does not translate into openness of the governance layer. The authors interpret this as a direct extension of Michels into a domain that was supposed to falsify him. Peer production is not a refutation of the iron law; it is another instance of it.

Documented rules, appeal mechanisms, and transparent logs do not, by themselves, neutralize this drift. A community can have all of them and still concentrate decisive authority among early and self-selecting members who understand the process apparatus better than anyone else. The apparatus meant to support open participation can become the terrain on which insider advantage is exercised: familiarity with procedure is itself a resource, unevenly distributed, and it compounds over time. Established members occupy the positions from which authority operates; the process feels coherent to them because they are fluent in it and, often, because they shape its application. Peripheral members encounter the same process as opaque machinery whose operators are not accountable through any mechanism they can reach. The gap between core and periphery is not a perception error; it tracks a real distribution of power.

Shaw and Hill's contribution is to show that the question of who actually governs is not answered by the presence of open participation or documented procedure. Process design is itself a site where oligarchic dynamics take hold, and the work of distributing authority — rotating roles, lowering the cost of meaningful participation for newcomers, surfacing decisions that would otherwise be made invisibly by the core — is a distinct problem from the work of making rules legible. Peer production inherits, rather than escapes, the organizational dynamics Michels identified a century earlier.
