# Quarantined! Examining the Effects of a Community-Wide Moderation Intervention on Reddit

## Abstract
Should social media platforms override a community's self-policing when it repeatedly break rules? What actions can they consider? In light of this debate, platforms have begun experimenting with softer alternatives to outright bans. We examine one such intervention called quarantining, that impedes direct access to and promotion of controversial communities. Specifically, we present two case studies of what happened when Reddit quarantined the influential communities r/TheRedPill (TRP) and r/The_Donald (TD). Using over 85M Reddit posts, we apply causal inference methods to examine the quarantine's effects on TRP and TD. We find that the quarantine made it more difficult to recruit new members: new user influx to TRP and TD decreased by 79.5% and 58%, respectively. Despite quarantining, existing users' misogyny and racism levels remained unaffected. We conclude by reflecting on the effectiveness of this design friction in limiting the influence of toxic communities and discuss broader implications for content moderation.

## AI Summary
Chandrasekharan, Jhaver, Bruckman, and Gilbert (2022) studied Reddit's quarantine intervention as a middle-ground moderation approach positioned between allowing toxic communities to operate freely and outright banning them. The quarantine mechanism works by adding friction to community access: quarantined subreddits are removed from Reddit's recommendation systems, require users to explicitly opt in to view content, and display warning interstitials before entry. This study examined two high-profile quarantines -- r/TheRedPill (TRP), a community associated with misogynistic ideology, and r/The_Donald (TD), a community associated with political extremism and racism.

The researchers assembled a dataset of over 85 million Reddit posts and applied causal inference methods to rigorously examine the effects of quarantining on these two communities. Causal inference techniques allowed the authors to move beyond simple before-and-after comparisons and more confidently attribute observed changes to the quarantine intervention itself rather than to confounding factors or general trends on the platform.

The study produced two key findings that together paint a nuanced picture of quarantining's effects. First, quarantining dramatically reduced new member recruitment. The influx of new users to TRP decreased by 79.5%, and new user influx to TD decreased by 58%. This demonstrates that the added friction of quarantining is highly effective at preventing community growth -- potential new members who might have casually discovered these communities through Reddit's recommendation and browsing systems were largely cut off from doing so.

Second, however, existing members' levels of misogyny and racism remained unaffected by the quarantine. Users who were already participating in these communities before the quarantine continued to express the same levels of hateful content afterward. The quarantine did not prompt any measurable behavioral change among established community members.

This combination of findings suggests that quarantining is effective at containment -- limiting a toxic community's growth, reach, and ability to attract new members -- but not at reform -- changing the attitudes or behaviors of existing members. This is an important distinction for platform governance, as it clarifies what different enforcement tools can and cannot accomplish. Platforms deploying quarantines should understand that they are reducing a community's influence and preventing its expansion, not rehabilitating its current participants.

The paper contributes to a broader understanding of "design friction" as a moderation strategy. Design friction refers to deliberate obstacles placed in a user's path to slow down or discourage certain behaviors. In the context of content moderation, quarantining represents a calibrated application of friction that stops short of the nuclear option of banning while still meaningfully constraining a community's reach. The authors discuss the implications of this approach for platform policy, noting that different moderation goals -- containment versus reform versus elimination -- may require different tools. Quarantining appears well-suited to containment goals but should not be expected to serve as a reformative intervention. The study provides empirical grounding for platform decision-makers weighing the tradeoffs between various moderation strategies along the spectrum from inaction to permanent removal.
