# Technological Frames and User Innovation: Exploring Technological Change in Community Moderation Teams

Kiene, C., Jiang, J. A., & Hill, B. M. (2019). Technological Frames and User Innovation: Exploring Technological Change in Community Moderation Teams. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 3(CSCW), Article 44. https://doi.org/10.1145/3359146

## Abstract

Management of technological change in organizations is one of the most enduring topics in the literature on computer-supported cooperative work. The successful navigation of technological change is both more challenging and more critical in online communities that are entirely mediated by technology than it is in traditional organizations. This paper presents an analysis of 14 in-depth interviews with moderators of subcommunities of one technological platform (Reddit) that added communities on a new technological platform (Discord). Moderation teams experienced several problems related to moderating content at scale as well as a disconnect between the affordances of Discord and their assumptions based on their experiences on Reddit. We found that moderation teams used Discord's API to create scripts and bots that augmented Discord to make the platform work more like tools on Reddit. These tools were particularly important in communities struggling with scale. Our findings suggest that increasingly widespread end user programming allow users of social computing systems to innovate and deploy solutions to unanticipated design problems by transforming new technological platforms to align with their past expectations.

## AI Summary

Kiene, Jiang, and Hill (2019) study what happens when the volunteer moderators of established online communities add a second technological platform to their existing one and attempt to carry their governance practices across. The empirical setting is eight communities that began as subreddits and later opened Discord servers to host synchronous discussion alongside their Reddit forums. Drawing on 14 in-depth interviews with representatives of these moderation teams, the authors trace how moderators approached Discord through expectations formed on Reddit, discovered gaps between those expectations and what Discord actually afforded, and built their own tools to close the gaps. The study is grounded theoretically in Wanda Orlikowski's concept of *technological frames* — the shared assumptions, expectations, and knowledge a group uses to make sense of a technology — and in Eric von Hippel's work on *user innovation*, which treats users as creators of new technology rather than as passive adopters.

The moderators' accounts make clear that Reddit and Discord afford different kinds of governance work. Reddit provides a built-in suite of moderation tools: AutoModerator, a customizable bot that filters content by words, URLs, and patterns; a Mod Log that records moderator actions; and Modmail, a shared mailbox through which the moderation team communicates with community members. Discord, at the time of the study, provided a far thinner set of native affordances — an Audit Log that recorded moderator actions but could not store context on why a member was sanctioned, an Explicit Content Filter that could not be customized, and no shared inbox for moderator-to-member correspondence. The synchronous chat format also meant content moved faster and left less time for deliberate review than Reddit's forum-style posting.

The authors document how these architectural differences became acute as communities grew. In small Discord servers, moderators described light workloads and few complaints about the built-in tools. In large servers — sometimes tens or hundreds of thousands of members — the native tooling broke down: moderators could not track member histories, could not enforce rules consistently across shifts, and could not keep up with the volume of content. The moderators' response was not to accept the limits of the platform but to build around them. Using Discord's API, moderation teams deployed and configured bots that filtered content with customizable regular expressions, logged moderator actions with justifications into private channels that functioned as searchable member histories, handled bulk message deletion, and approximated the functions Reddit provided natively. Moderators explicitly modeled these constructions on the Reddit tools they already knew, describing bot-based filters as their "AutoModerator" and bot-logged channels as their "mod logs."

Two theoretical observations follow. First, technological frames continue to operate: moderators interpreted a new platform through the conceptual vocabulary of an old one, and this shaped what they noticed as missing and what they chose to build. Second, end user programming changes what frames produce. In Orlikowski's original formulation, users reconstructed technology socially — through altered practices and meanings layered over a fixed artifact. Here, users reconstructed the artifact itself, writing code that changed what the platform did. The authors argue this is an increasingly common pattern in social computing systems that expose APIs and support bots: users do not only reinterpret tools, they rebuild them.

The study thus offers a detailed empirical picture of governance infrastructure as a hybrid product. Part of it is what a platform ships with; part of it is what volunteer administrators assemble externally out of scripts, bots, shared configuration, and accumulated operational knowledge. The moderators interviewed were not merely users of Discord; they were maintainers of a custom tooling layer whose existence was invisible from outside the moderation team but whose presence was necessary for the community to function at scale. The paper documents this labor carefully and treats it as central rather than incidental to how moderated communities actually work.
