# Pride and Professionalization in Volunteer Moderation: Lessons for Effective Platform-User Collaboration

Seering, J., Dym, B., Kaufman, G., & Bernstein, M. (2022). Pride and Professionalization in Volunteer Moderation: Lessons for Effective Platform-User Collaboration. *Journal of Online Trust and Safety*, 1(2). https://tsjournal.org/index.php/jots/article/view/34

## Abstract

While most moderation actions on major social platforms are performed by either the platforms themselves or volunteer moderators, it is rare for platforms to collaborate directly with moderators to address problems. This paper examines how the group-chatting platform Discord coordinated with experienced volunteer moderators to respond to hate and harassment toward LGBTQ+ communities during Pride Month, June 2021, in what came to be known as the "Pride Mod" initiative. Representatives from Discord and volunteer moderators collaboratively identified and communicated with targeted communities, and volunteers temporarily joined servers that requested support to supplement those servers' existing volunteer moderation teams. Though LGBTQ+ communities were subject to a wave of targeted hate during Pride Month, the communities that received the requested volunteer support reported having a better capacity to handle the issues that arose. This paper reports the results of interviews with 11 moderators who participated in the initiative as well as the Discord employee who coordinated it. We show how this initiative was made possible by the way Discord has cultivated trust and built formal connections with its most active volunteers, and discuss the ethical implications of formal collaborations between for-profit platforms and volunteer users.

## AI Summary

Seering, Dym, Kaufman, and Bernstein (2022) examine an unusual episode: the "Pride Mod" initiative of June 2021, in which Discord's internal staff coordinated with a pool of experienced volunteer moderators to provide temporary reinforcement to LGBTQ+ servers facing coordinated harassment during Pride Month. The paper's method is qualitative — semi-structured interviews with eleven participating moderators and with the Discord employee who organized the program — and its analytic focus is less on the anti-harassment outcomes than on the social infrastructure that made the collaboration possible at all. What the authors find, in tracing how Discord was able to mobilize trusted volunteers on short notice, is that a recognizable occupational subculture of volunteer moderators already existed on the platform, with its own conventions, vocabularies, and internal status markers, and that the platform's program leaned on that subculture rather than creating it.

A central observation concerns the language the interviewees used to describe their own work. Moderators spoke about their practice in terms that borrowed heavily from professional and paraprofessional registers — training, intake, escalation, handoff, coverage — and drew distinctions among themselves that were essentially credentialing distinctions, even though no formal credential existed. Seniority was tracked in years of service across servers, in the size and difficulty of servers moderated, and in participation in cross-server moderator communities where techniques, war stories, and ethical norms circulated. The authors describe this as a process of *professionalization*: an unpaid role hardening into something with an identifiable craft identity, occupational pride, and an internal hierarchy legible to insiders but largely invisible from outside the moderator layer.

The interviews also surface a strong attachment to *pride in craft*. Participants expressed satisfaction at being trusted with difficult situations, at handling incidents their peers would recognize as hard, and at being part of an informal network whose members know each other by reputation. That pride is generative — it sustains unpaid labor over years — but the authors are careful to note its other face. The same dynamics that cohere the moderator community also regulate entry into it. Newcomers to moderation, and moderators of smaller or less prestigious servers, occupy a different standing than members of the cross-server network Discord could draw on for the initiative. Access to the more consequential forms of the role runs through social ties, reputational signals, and familiarity with a shared vocabulary that is not documented anywhere, and whose existence is not announced to those outside it.

The paper draws out the structural tension in this arrangement. Volunteer moderation is usually framed as community self-governance: communities police themselves, and the people who do that work are peers of the moderated. What the interviews reveal is that the moderator layer on a large platform is not flatly continuous with the user base; it is a partially separate social world with its own norms of membership, its own mechanisms for conferring standing, and its own criteria — largely tacit — for deciding whose judgment counts. The Pride Mod initiative worked because Discord could reach into that world and activate existing relationships. The authors treat this as a genuine achievement of the initiative and simultaneously as an ethical question: a for-profit platform is able to summon unpaid labor quickly because it has cultivated relationships with a semi-professional volunteer elite, and the terms of membership in that elite are set informally within the moderator community itself.

The authors resist a simple reading. They are not arguing that professionalization is bad; the pride, the shared standards, and the accumulated expertise are what made an effective response possible. But they are clear that the collaboration's conditions of possibility include a layer of informal structure — who is known, who is trusted, who speaks the language — that operates upstream of any decision the platform makes about whom to invite. That layer is durable, consequential, and largely undocumented, and the paper's contribution is to make it visible as an object of study.
