# Lawless

Suzor, N. P. (2019). *Lawless: The Secret Rules That Govern Our Digital Lives*. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108666428

## Abstract

Rampant abuse, hate speech, censorship, bias, and disinformation — our Internet has problems. It is governed by technology companies — search engines, social media platforms, and infrastructure providers — whose hidden rules influence what we are allowed to see and say. In *Lawless*, Nicolas P. Suzor presents gripping examples of exactly how tech companies govern our digital environment and how they bend to pressure from governments and other powerful actors to censor and control the flow of information online. We are at a constitutional moment — an opportunity to rethink the basic rules of how the Internet is governed. Suzor offers a vision of a vibrant, diverse, and flourishing internet that can protect our fundamental rights from the lawless rule of tech. The culmination of more than ten years of original research, this groundbreaking work should be read by anyone who cares about the internet and the future of our shared social spaces. (Publisher description, Cambridge University Press.)

## AI Summary

Suzor (2019) argues that the platforms now mediating most public communication operate as governors — setting and enforcing the rules under which billions of people speak, organize, and associate — while lacking the constraints that societies have historically required of institutions exercising such power. The organizing analytic is the rule of law: a set of procedural expectations developed over centuries to discipline arbitrary authority. Suzor's central move is to take that familiar apparatus and hold it up against the actual operation of terms of service, content moderation systems, account enforcement, and ranking decisions. The mismatch is the book's subject.

The rule-of-law frame does specific work. It is not a demand that platforms become states, and it is not satisfied by the mere existence of published policies. It asks whether rules are knowable in advance, applied consistently across like cases, explained to the people they affect, and subject to review by someone other than the party that made them. Suzor's documentation of platform practice — account suspensions executed without stated reasons, content removals whose logic shifts with public pressure or political context, appeal channels that route back to the same decision-makers, policies whose meaning is disclosed only through enforcement — shows these conditions systematically absent. The result is a form of private authority that governs heavily but is not accountable in any of the ways that would make its decisions legitimate under the tradition it superficially resembles.

A key contribution is the insistence that this lawlessness is not incidental but architectural. Terms of service reserve nearly unlimited discretion. Automated systems act at a speed and scale that forecloses individualized review. Opacity is not a bug but a feature of business models that depend on inscrutable ranking and on the capacity to remove users without negotiation. Technical infrastructure — the capacity to deplatform, demonetize, shadow-ban, down-rank — creates a register of consequence that has no analogue in the civic procedures people expect when serious decisions are made about them. Suzor draws out how these technical and contractual mechanisms together constitute a *legal order* in substance, one that users are bound by but cannot effectively contest.

The normative claim follows from the descriptive one. If platforms are in fact governing — and Suzor's evidence that they are is dense and varied — then the rule-of-law standards that apply to other governors should apply to them. That does not mean replicating courts; it means insisting on procedural minima: *notice* of rules before they are enforced, *consistency* in how rules are applied across cases, *reasoned decisions* so affected users can understand what happened to them, and some form of *independent review* so enforcement is not the last word of the enforcer. These are not luxury features. They are the conditions under which authority earns the cooperation of the governed rather than extracting compliance through the asymmetry of its position.

Suzor is careful not to romanticize the alternative. He acknowledges that moderation at scale is genuinely difficult, that platforms face real pressure from states and publics, and that no rule system will satisfy every constituency. But the response to difficulty cannot be the abandonment of procedural discipline altogether. The book's second half sketches what a *digital constitutionalism* might look like: a layered set of commitments — inside firms, across industry, through external oversight, and in law — that would make platform power answerable in the specific ways rule-of-law traditions have developed to make power answerable. The contribution is to show that the vocabulary already exists, that the deficits are nameable, and that the contractual and architectural arrangements presently foreclosing accountability are choices rather than necessities.
