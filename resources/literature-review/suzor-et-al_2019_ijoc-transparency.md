# What Do We Mean When We Talk About Transparency? Toward Meaningful Transparency in Commercial Content Moderation

Suzor, N. P., West, S. M., Quodling, A., & York, J. (2019). What Do We Mean When We Talk About Transparency? Toward Meaningful Transparency in Commercial Content Moderation. *International Journal of Communication*, 13, 1526–1543.

## Abstract

This paper seeks to provide greater specificity to demands for transparency in the commercial content moderation practices of digital platforms. We identify gaps in knowledge through a thematic analysis of 380 survey responses from individuals who have been the subject of content moderation decisions. We argue that meaningful transparency should be understood as a component of a communicative process of accountability (rendering account) to independent stakeholders. We make specific recommendations for platforms to provide people with clear information about decisions that affect them, including what content is moderated, which rule was breached, and a description of the people and automated processes responsible for identifying content and making the decision. Beyond providing more information to individuals about particular decisions, however, we note the major challenge of improving understanding of content moderation at a systems level. General demands for greater transparency should be reframed to focus on enhanced access to large-scale disaggregated data that can enable new methods and collaborations between academia, civil society, and journalists to make these systems more understandable and accountable.

## AI Summary

Suzor, West, Quodling, and York (2019) intervene in a policy conversation that by the late 2010s had converged on "transparency" as a near-universal remedy for the problems of commercial content moderation. Platforms had begun publishing community guidelines, issuing periodic transparency reports with takedown counts and government-request tallies, and releasing enforcement statistics. The authors argue that this accumulation of disclosure has not produced what transparency is supposed to produce. People subject to moderation decisions still do not understand why those decisions were made, cannot effectively contest them, and have no reliable way to evaluate whether the rules are being applied consistently. The paper's central move is to treat "transparency" not as a single value that platforms have more or less of, but as a bundle of distinct requirements that can be satisfied independently — and that, when satisfied unevenly, produce the appearance of openness without its substance.

The empirical basis is a thematic analysis of 380 responses to a survey of people who had been the subject of content moderation actions — account suspensions, post removals, demotions, and related sanctions. What the authors find, reading across these accounts, is a recurring pattern: users report being told that a rule was broken without being told which rule, being unable to identify what specific content triggered the action, being routed through appeal mechanisms that yield no explanation, and being unable to distinguish automated decisions from human ones. The information deficit is not primarily about aggregate statistics. It is about the individual decision: its grounds, its author, and the avenues available for response.

From this base, the authors develop a distinction that organizes the rest of the argument. On one side sits a thin form of transparency — publishing policies, releasing numbers, issuing reports — which platforms can perform without enabling any affected party to understand or challenge what happened to them. On the other side sits meaningful transparency, which the authors frame as a communicative process of rendering account to those affected and to independent stakeholders capable of evaluating the account given. Meaningful transparency requires that information be intelligible to the person receiving it, specific enough to support contestation, and connected to mechanisms through which the account can actually be tested. Disclosure that cannot be understood, or that describes the system at a level of abstraction that obscures any particular decision, does not qualify — even when its volume is large.

The paper's recommendations follow from this reframing. At the individual level, platforms should tell affected users what content was moderated, which specific rule was applied, and whether the decision was made by a person or by an automated process (and, where automated, something about how). Appeal routes should produce substantive responses rather than restatements of the original outcome. At the systems level, the authors argue that the current transparency-report format — headline numbers released on the platform's own terms — cannot support the kind of evaluation that external researchers, civil society organizations, and journalists need to do. What is required instead is access to large-scale disaggregated data that makes patterns of enforcement inspectable by parties whose job is to inspect them. Accountability is relational: it exists only when someone capable of holding the platform to account can actually see what the platform is doing.

Running through the argument is a claim about legitimacy. A moderation regime can publish everything it claims to publish, produce reports on a predictable schedule, and still fail the people it acts upon — because disclosure is not the same as comprehension, and comprehension is not the same as the capacity to contest. Where users cannot make sense of decisions that affect them and have no means of pressing back, the governance relationship has not been rendered legitimate by the act of publishing; it has merely been documented.
