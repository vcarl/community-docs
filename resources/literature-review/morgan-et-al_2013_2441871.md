# Tea and Sympathy: Crafting Positive New User Experiences on Wikipedia

Morgan, J. T., Bouterse, S., Walls, H., & Stierch, S. (2013). Tea and Sympathy: Crafting Positive New User Experiences on Wikipedia. *Proceedings of CSCW 2013*, 839–848. https://doi.org/10.1145/2441776.2441871

## Abstract

The following description is drawn from general knowledge of this paper and from the Wikipedia Teahouse pilot report and should be treated as a paraphrase rather than the publisher's verbatim abstract.*

The paper reports on the design and initial evaluation of the Wikipedia Teahouse, a pilot project intended to improve the early experience of new editors — especially those from groups historically underrepresented on Wikipedia, most visibly women. The authors describe the Teahouse's design principles, including a deliberately informal tone, visible human hosts, a simplified question-and-answer interface, and targeted personalized invitations sent to promising new contributors. They evaluate the pilot using a mixed-methods approach combining logged editor activity, newcomer surveys, and comparison against matched control groups of non-invited newcomers. They report that invited editors who engaged with the Teahouse were retained and remained active at substantially higher rates than comparable newcomers who did not receive an invitation, and that the initiative drew a larger proportion of women editors than Wikipedia's overall editor base. The authors argue that lightweight, designed spaces for early socialization can meaningfully shift the trajectory of newcomer participation in a large open-collaboration community.

## AI Summary

Morgan, Bouterse, Walls, and Stierch (2013) report on the design and early evaluation of the Wikipedia Teahouse, a dedicated space on English Wikipedia intended to change the encounter between new editors and the established community. The paper's starting observation is that Wikipedia had a well-documented newcomer problem — declining retention, a narrow demographic profile, and a reputation among would-be contributors for being hostile and procedurally opaque — and that the informal welcoming practices that did exist on the site were not reaching most of the people they needed to reach. Individual editors who took the time to greet newcomers warmly, answer basic questions patiently, and defuse early conflicts were doing real work, but that work was diffuse, depended on who happened to notice a new account, and in aggregate left large segments of the newcomer population — women in particular — without a visible point of entry. The authors frame the Teahouse as an attempt to take those scattered welcoming practices and give them a designed home: an explicit place, with explicit invitations, staffed by people who had explicitly agreed to play the role of host.

The design is described in some detail. The Teahouse has visible hosts with photos and introductions rather than anonymous helpers; recent questions and answers are surfaced prominently so that activity is legible to a visitor arriving for the first time; the page uses a visual and tonal register distinct from the rest of Wikipedia's help infrastructure, trading policy links and wiki jargon for plain language and a warmer aesthetic; and a custom question-posting interface lets newcomers ask for help without having to learn wiki markup. Invitations to the Teahouse are not left to chance. The team identifies promising new editors through database reports on recent activity and sends each of them a personalized, friendly invitation message, so that arrival at the Teahouse is an outcome of outreach rather than navigation.

The evaluation is mixed-methods. The authors combine behavioral data from edit logs with survey responses from participating newcomers and qualitative observation of interactions in the space, and they compare invited-and-participating newcomers against matched control groups of similar newcomers who did not receive invitations. The behavioral results are the part most often cited: invited editors who engaged with the Teahouse were retained at notably higher rates than matched controls over the observed window, edited more articles, and made more edits overall. The composition of participating newcomers also differed from the Wikipedia baseline, with a substantially higher share of women than the site's overall editor population. Survey responses indicated high satisfaction with the experience and, importantly, a sense among participants that the Teahouse felt qualitatively different from other parts of Wikipedia.

The broader contribution of the paper is to treat newcomer welcome not as a matter of individual virtue or cultural tone but as something that can be designed. The informal norms of helpful, patient engagement that Wikipedia's best contributors were already practicing on their own were converted into an explicit setting — with roles, invitations, a distinctive interface, and a staffed calendar — and the measurable result was that more newcomers stayed and that the population of newcomers who stayed looked different from the population already there. The paper's value as a case is that it shows the intermediate step clearly: a community identified a welcoming practice that some of its members were already performing tacitly, built a scaffolded space that made that practice legible and routable, and documented that the scaffolded version reached and retained people the tacit version had been missing.
